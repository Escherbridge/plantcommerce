**Kratky (non-circulating) hydroponics and its limits**

# The Kratky method: non-circulating hydroponics

The University of Hawaiʻi method that runs without electricity or pumps works because the crop is finished before the tank is empty, and because an air gap — not an air stone — feeds the “oxygen roots.” B.A. Kratky already wrote that down. The charm is that there is no pump. The catch is written down too: this is not a general-purpose hydroponic system, and it is not “DWC without a pump.” This page is about the published limits, not about a mason jar.

## Hero figures

- **UH–CTAHR VC-1 (September 2010):** “The entire crop of lettuce is grown with a single initial application of water and nutrients. Electricity and pumps are not needed.” Figure 1’s caption: “Electricity, pumps, and wicks are not needed. All of the nutrient solution is applied prior to transplanting.” The method “can be scaled to home-garden use.”
- **Crop-water cap, same publication:** the method “is intended only for crops that require less than 2 gallons of water per plant for the entire growing season, from transplanting to harvest.” Potential crops listed: leafy, romaine, and semi-head lettuces, cilantro, green onions, kai choy, pak choi, and watercress.
- **2009 *Acta Horticulturae* paper:** leaf and semi-head lettuce cultivars in the darkened 4-liter bottle description “are usually harvested at about 6 to 7 weeks after seeding.” Plants in that small-bottle example “typically require less than 4 liters of water for their lifespan” (about 1.06 gallons, inside the later VC-1 cap). That is a worked example, not a guarantee for every crop.

## How the air gap works

VC-1 describes polyethylene-lined tanks 5½ inches deep, “filled nearly to the top with nutrient solution,” with a cover that has holes. At transplant, net pots with medium or grow-plugs and 1- to 3-week-old lettuce seedlings are set in the holes; “the lower ½ inch or so of each net pot is immersed,” and the medium moistens by capillary action. Within a few weeks the solution level drops below the net pots. Roots in the solution take up water and nutrients, while roots in the humid air layer become “oxygen roots.” “The crop is harvested before the nutrient solution is exhausted. Then, the tank is cleaned and refilled.”

The 2009 paper restates three non-circulating methods, all with the entire nutrient solution added before planting or transplanting, and no electricity, pumps, or wicks. The simplest is lettuce seeded in a tapered net pot, placed in a darkened 4-liter bottle filled with nutrient solution, with the lower 3 cm of the pot immersed. A commercial-scale version uses a 14 cm high tank lined with polyethylene, covered with an expanded or extruded polystyrene sheet. A third, float-support version uses long raceway tanks: the cover initially floats, then comes to rest on two parallel 10 cm plastic pipes as the solution recedes; the tank is filled with water immediately before harvest so rafts can be moved.

A home-garden summary also exists from UC Master Gardeners of Orange County. Any figure on this page comes from the UH papers, not from that summary.

## Rain, sun, and heat

Rain shelters are required in rainy locations. Without them, VC-1 says, “rainfall enters the growing tanks and raises the liquid level. This causes roots that had been suspended in the moist air zone above the water to become submerged and starved for oxygen. Plants initially wilt and may suffer physiological damage or even ‘drown’ and die.” Rain shelters in that publication are typically a frame with polyethylene or rigid plastic, sometimes screened sides; they “do not contain active heating or cooling devices and do not need electrical power,” but usually include a water supply. Use only UV-stabilized greenhouse-grade film, “rather than construction-grade polyethylene, which will fail within 6 months of exposure to sunlight.”

That Hawaiʻi rain-shelter note is not an Intermountain winter design. A sealed tank in a hot hoop still has a heat problem. [Hydroponics in the Intermountain West](/guides/hydroponics-intermountain) and the [ventilation and heat](/guides/greenhouse-ventilation-heat) guide are the places for that climate and for opening the sides. [Deep water culture](/guides/deep-water-culture) is the aerated alternative, with official dissolved-oxygen floors; if you add an air stone to a Kratky tank, you are no longer running the method the papers describe. Nutrient targets still belong on the [EC and pH](/guides/hydroponic-ec-ph-nutrients) page, and the comparison among system types is in [choosing a first hydroponic system](/guides/choosing-hydroponic-system).

## Common mistakes

Using the method for tomatoes or other high-water fruiting crops, outside the less-than-2-gallon cap and the listed crop set, is the first. Topping the tank up and submerging the oxygen roots, or letting rain do it, is the exact failure VC-1 describes. Covering the tank with construction-grade plastic and expecting it to last a season is the third (failure within 6 months in sun, per VC-1). Never cleaning the tank between crops is the fourth. Calling this DWC, or adding an air stone and then wondering why the published method no longer describes the build, is the fifth.

## What to do with the usual questions

**What is the Kratky method?** A suspended net pot over a non-circulating reservoir, as originated in Kratky’s UH work. A darkened bottle is the simplest published form (2009 paper); tanks and raceways are the scale-up. Lettuce is the crop the papers are written around.

**Does it need a pump?** No. That is the point of VC-1, and also the reason for the crop-water and rain limits.

**Can I grow anything in a Kratky jar?** Not according to the inventor. VC-1: crops that use less than 2 gallons per plant, with a short listed set.

## An illustrative scenario

This is a worked example, not a customer and not a testimonial. A tomato is planted in a jar, the tank is topped up so the air roots stay underwater, and the jar is left out in the rain. That is the failure VC-1 already describes, plus a crop the method was not intended for.
