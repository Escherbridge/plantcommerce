**Hydroponics in a high-desert climate**

# What actually constrains a closed growing system in the Intermountain West

Running a closed indoor or hoop-house grow in the Treasure Valley is nothing like following generic humid-coastal blog charts. Winter, in the official map, is a zone 7a problem in central Boise, and a 6b problem in the foothills. Summer is a 90°F-day problem. Annual precipitation at the official Boise airport station is 11.51 inches, with July and August almost dry. A closed, soilless loop still has to live in that weather. The hard part is not “saving water in the bed.” It is heat, winter nights, source-water salts, and the water you spend on cooling. Sound planning starts with matching water, climate, and power to the site.

## The bill for a split system

A closed loop is often sold as if the climate had been edited out, but it has not. Three constraints sit on the same bench, and each one has a public number.

**Heat.** At Boise Air Terminal, the July mean daily maximum is 92.7°F, and there are 54.8 days a year at or above 90°F (NOAA, 1991–2020 normals). Inside a structure, the number can be worse. Utah State University Extension recorded temperatures above 120°F on a sunny day in early March in a Northern Utah high tunnel, when high and low tunnels were not properly ventilated. Those tunnels, in the same Intermountain guidance, are usually passively heated and cooled; ventilation on sunny days is required to stay below a crop’s heat-injury range. Cooling is not optional, and March is already late enough to prove it.

**Water, including the cooling water.** In a Tucson, Arizona, single-span polyethylene greenhouse of hydroponic tomatoes (March–October 2006), Sabeh, Giacomelli, and Kubota measured eight-month irrigation water use of 780 L/m² and pad-and-fan cooling of 1,450 L/m² — more water on cooling than on the crop. Irrigation rose from 4.3 L/m²/day at establishment to 7.2 L/m²/day at maturity; pad-and-fan peaked at 11 L/m²/day in the hottest, driest conditions. Water-use efficiency was 30 kg yield per m³ for irrigation alone, 16 for cooling, and 11 when both were combined. That Tucson study serves as a helpful semi-arid benchmark for desert and Intermountain summer evaporation. University of Arizona CEAC teaching notes give a working example for greenhouse hydroponic tomatoes of about 4 liters per plant per day, or 10 liters/m²/day at 2.5 plants/m², and state that if evaporative cooling is used, especially in deserts, water needs may double. The chapter presents the 4 L/plant/day line as an example, not a universal rule. Always budget evaporative cooling water alongside crop irrigation when sizing dry-climate storage.

**Winter nights and salt.** The 2023 USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map uses 30-year averages of the lowest annual winter temperatures. ZIP 83702 is zone 7a; ZIP 83716 is zone 6b. USDA is explicit that a designation is the average annual extreme minimum, not the coldest it has ever been or will be, and is a general guide for perennials, not a guarantee. Source water quality — pH, EC / salt levels, contaminants — is listed by CEAC as a constraint before any nutrient recipe.

## A shared record, applied here

**Size the water budget for cooling, not only for the reservoir.** If a pad-and-fan or other evaporative system is in the design, treat makeup water as a first-class line, in the spirit of the Tucson measurements and the CEAC desert-cooling note. A reservoir sized only for the plants is a reservoir sized for half the problem.

**Vent and shade as if March can already overheat a tunnel.** That is what the USU Extension record says happened when ventilation was not done. A sunny Intermountain day is not a gentle warmup just because the calendar still says early spring.

**Test source water, and look up the parcel’s 2023 half-zone.** Measure pH and EC before writing a recipe. Check the ZIP, including the 6b pockets in the foothills (83716). The map is a 30-year average of extreme minima, not a forecast of the next cold night.
