**Harvesting rainwater for garden irrigation**

# Harvesting rainwater for garden irrigation

A roof is a catchment, not a filter. Official rainwater-harvesting bulletins already describe a roof-to-tank system, and they already say to keep that water on the soil — not on the edible leaf — and to size storage from rainfall and demand, not from a pretty barrel. Roof water is not automatically clean water, and a cistern size is a water budget, not a number this page will invent for Boise. This is a catchment-and-use guide for outdoor irrigation, not a hydroponic recipe. What to test in irrigation water still lives on the [source-water testing](/guides/source-water-testing) page.

## The system, labeled Purdue 2024

Purdue Extension / Illinois-Indiana Sea Grant WQ40, *Rainwater Harvesting for Homes and Community Gardens*, October 2024, Sara Winnike McMillan (Iowa State) and Kara Salazar (Purdue): rainwater harvesting “collects rainfall from a rooftop or other structure to be stored and used later for irrigation or other beneficial uses.” Six components: catchment, conveyance (gutters, filters), storage, treatment (opaque or covered tanks to limit algae; first-flush diversion), overflow, and distribution. “Rain barrels typically hold 55 gallons, but cisterns come in a range of sizes.” “The optimal size is best approximated by performing a water budget.” Official supply estimate, labeled Purdue 2024: “Harvested rainwater (gallons) = catchment area (ft²) × rainfall depth (inches) × 0.62 (conversion factor) × 0.85 (collection efficiency)” because “evaporative loss and inefficiencies can reduce total volume by up to 15%.” That formula is a method, not a Boise cistern size. A reader can plug in a local rainfall depth from a cited climate source; this page does not invent a local total. First-flush diverters “are designed to capture approximately 10 gallons for every 1,000 square feet of catchment surface.” Avoid catchment surfaces that can leach problems: the publication flags untreated metal such as galvanized roof (zinc), treated wood, and lead-based paint; “asphalt shingles are suitable if these are in good condition.” Food-safety practices: minimize contamination of collected water, and use “watering approaches to reduce contact with edible portions of the plant,” including not using harvested water to wash produce, and maximizing time between irrigation and harvest. Inspect gutters; use screens; label non-potable water. Fifty-five gallons is a typical barrel, not “enough for the garden.”

## Using it on food, labeled Rutgers and Purdue

Rutgers Cooperative Extension Fact Sheet FS1218, “Rain Barrels Part IV: Testing and Applying Harvested Water to Irrigate a Vegetable Garden,” Michele Bakacs, Michael Haberland, and Steve Yergeau. The live page footer is Copyright © 2026 Rutgers; the study described in the fact sheet is Haberland, Bakacs & Yergeau, 2013. Those results are a New Jersey rain-barrel study, not Intermountain roof chemistry. In that study, heavy metals were “well below federal irrigation standards for reclaimed water”; PAHs were not detected; “the majority of water samples” were below recommended irrigation guidelines for *E. coli*, while *E. coli* was present in 66% of samples against New Jersey drinking-water standards, and 9% of samples exceeded the federal irrigation single-sample *E. coli* limit (Table 2 on the fact sheet). Practical application, labeled Rutgers: apply harvested water “to the soil, possibly through drip irrigation,” not “directly on the plant”; “water should be applied in the morning only”; do not harvest immediately after watering. Drip in a soil bed is the subject of the [soil drip irrigation](/guides/soil-drip-irrigation) guide.

The fact sheet also describes a household-bleach disinfection option and states, in the same passage, that “household bleach is not labeled for use in water treatment by the Food and Drug Administration.” This page does not turn that option into an Aevani dosing recipe. If disinfection is mentioned, it is Rutgers’ option with its FDA-label caveat, not an instruction. Rutgers found frequent lab testing “impractical and too expensive for the average homeowner” as a substitute for practices; that is their conclusion about frequency, not a reason to skip a test when a rule or a worried grower needs one. Do not treat roof runoff as automatically softer or cleaner than a well.

USDA NRCS High Tunnel System 325 Overview (March 2015) lists associated practices including Roof Runoff Structure (Code 558) and Underground Outlet (Code 620). Those are practice names on the opened Overview. This page does not print payment rates, program eligibility, or grant deadlines. The full CPS 325 PDF was not retrieved in this library’s source pass. High tunnels themselves are on the [high tunnels](/guides/high-tunnels-hoop-houses) guide. This brief is outdoor irrigation. It does not invent a rainwater hydroponic recipe the opened pages do not give.

## Common mistakes

Inventing a cistern size, or treating 55 gallons as “enough for the garden,” is the first. Spraying barrel water on edible leaves is the second (Rutgers, Purdue). Washing harvest with harvested water is the third (Purdue). An unscreened, translucent tank is the fourth. Turning the page into a bleach recipe, or ignoring Rutgers’ FDA-label caveat, is the fifth. Quoting NRCS payment rates is the sixth.

## What to do with the usual questions

**Is rainwater safe for vegetable gardens, and how big a cistern do I need?** Official leaning: usable for irrigation if you design and use it carefully; apply to soil, not foliage; size from a water budget (Purdue’s formula is a method). Rutgers’ New Jersey barrels were generally within irrigation metal limits, with some *E. coli* detections against the standards in that table — not a Treasure Valley lab report.

**Can I use it in hydroponics?** This page is outdoor irrigation. Source-water testing still applies. This page does not invent a rainwater hydroponic recipe.

**Do I need to bleach the barrel?** Rutgers describes a method and notes that household bleach is not FDA-labeled for water treatment. That is not this page’s instruction.
