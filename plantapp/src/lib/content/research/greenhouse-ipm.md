**Integrated pest management in greenhouses and tunnels**

# Integrated pest management in greenhouses and tunnels

A tunnel or greenhouse has its own pest complex, and official high-tunnel guides already say so. IPM here starts with scouting, sanitation, and clean transplants, not with a default spray. Penn State already said a tunnel is not the field. The first tool is a walk with a hand lens, not a bottle. This page is a scouting guide, not a pesticide brand list, and it does not invent economic thresholds that were not in the opened pages.

## Hero practices

Print both official scouting cadences rather than flattening them into one invented schedule.

- **Penn State Extension, “High Tunnel Production” (updated 13 March 2023):** pest management in a high tunnel “is different from growing the same crop in the open field. The complex of pests differs because of the controlled environment.” “To obtain long-term control… high-tunnel growers need to use an integrated pest management (IPM) approach.” “Monitoring to identify which insects, weeds, or diseases are present is the key.” “Daily scouting of plants and insect traps will provide you with the data you need.” “Pesticides should be used only when monitoring indicates they are absolutely needed.”
- **Penn State scouting article, “High Tunnel Vegetable Crops: Designing a Scouting Plan,” updated 25 October 2017:** scouting is “the cornerstone for successful integrated pest management (IPM).” Suggested starting frequency: “scout each tunnel once a week,” then adapt. Sticky cards: start with “1 sticky card per 100 feet of row” and adapt; yellow cards are attractive to most insects, blue to thrips. “Important not [to] rely only on sticky cards,” because aphids may not show there. Plant inspections: “scout 1 plant in each 20 feet long section of row, which equals 5 plants in a 100 feet long row, for spring and summer crops including tomato, pepper, eggplant, and cucumber.” For leafy greens: “2 plants in each 20 feet long section… or 10 in a 100 feet long row.” Scout every cultivar.
- **University of Kentucky Cooperative Extension ID-235 (revised October 2018):** inspect new plants coming into structures; maintain a 2-week plant-free period prior to each new crop cycle; thoroughly sanitize stakes and trellis materials between crops; eliminate weeds in and around structures (a “20-foot perimeter” is stated); use yellow sticky cards and check them regularly; record scouting observations.

A historic ATTRA greenhouse-IPM publication URL returned 404 in the research pass and is not cited here.

## The usual suspects, and what to do first

IPM, in Penn State’s production guide, “employs a combination of methods”: biological control, cultural controls, mechanical and physical controls, “and selective use of pesticides.” Exclusion of rain plus drip and mulches “greatly reduces weeds and diseases.” Ventilation lets many field insects in, “but often pests more commonly encountered in greenhouses are also present.” Primary diseases named there: powdery mildew (many crops) and leaf mold (tomatoes), “much more readily managed when vegetation is managed to provide for greater air movement.” That is why the [ventilation and heat](/guides/greenhouse-ventilation-heat) guide belongs next to this one, and why the [high tunnel](/guides/high-tunnels-hoop-houses) page treats sides and airflow as a job. Primary arthropod pests named: spider mites, aphids, and thrips. Inspect all transplants before planting and remove plants with aphids, whiteflies, thrips, lesions, or weeds in the trays.

The Penn State scouting article (photos and byline credit Elsa Sánchez) also describes plant-mediated systems: yellow marigolds as trap or indicator plants for thrips (and sometimes spider mites); bush beans as a trap plant for spider mites near tunnel edges; alyssum as a habitat plant for parasitic wasps and syrphid flies, with the caution that habitat plants can also attract unwanted pests. Natural enemies are often host-specific; identify the pest, and use a local educator or Penn State’s Insect ID Lab when unsure. The article describes missing an aphid outbreak on a small, susceptible pepper cultivar when the plan did not sample it.

University of Kentucky CCD-CP-63 (2025) already supplies lettuce disease notes used in the [root-rot troubleshooting](/guides/hydroponic-troubleshooting-root-rot) guide (Pythium, bacterial soft rot, bottom rot, powdery mildew, and the sanitation line “do not introduce diseased plugs”). Point there for water-culture root disease rather than reprinting the whole table.

## Pesticides last, and legal

UKY ID-235: “only apply pesticides as necessary”; “apply only those pesticides labeled both for the crop and for greenhouse use”; rotate modes of action; close and remove bumblebee colonies before using pollinator-toxic insecticides, storing bees in a shady location for a day before returning them. Biological control: “consider using” it for recurring pests; it “will not completely eliminate the pest(s)”; “correct identification of pest species is required.” This page is not a pesticide recommendation table. Send readers to the current label and to UKY’s photographic guide. No product names or rates are printed here, and UKY’s Kentucky scouting photos are not treated as an Idaho spray program.

## Common mistakes

Treating tunnel pests as identical to field pests, or as identical to a sealed greenhouse, is the first. Relying only on sticky cards, and missing aphids, is the second. Scouting the main cultivar and skipping the odd corner planting is the third. Spraying on a calendar, or with a product not labeled for greenhouse use, is the fourth. Carrying weeds and insects in on transplants, then wondering why IPM “doesn’t work,” is the fifth. Leaving bumblebees in the house during a pollinator-toxic application is the last, and UKY already wrote the storage step.

## What to do with the usual questions

**What are the common greenhouse pests and diseases?** Official starting lists: aphids, thrips, spider mites, and whiteflies on transplant inspection (Penn State names the first three as primary tunnel arthropods); powdery mildew and tomato leaf mold; plus water-culture root diseases in soilless systems. Identification first.

**How often should I scout?** Penn State production guide: daily scouting of plants and traps. Penn State scouting article: start once a week, then adapt. Print both sources; tighten the schedule from what you see, rather than inventing a hybrid cadence here.

**Do I need beneficial insects?** Official pages treat biological control as one IPM tool, used when the pest is identified and densities are still low (UKY). It “will not completely eliminate the pest(s).”
