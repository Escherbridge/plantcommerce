**Pollinating tomatoes in greenhouses and high tunnels**

# Pollinating tomatoes in greenhouses and high tunnels

A greenhouse is a windbreak you bought on purpose. Official high-tunnel pages already say tomato flowers need a jostle that field wind usually provides, and that purchased bumblebees, tapping, or a vibrator are how growers replace it. Temperature and humidity can abort the job even when something is buzzing. This page is for fruiting crops under plastic. Leaf lettuce does not need it. Official set points below are labeled by source; this page does not invent a tighter house band.

## How a tomato flower works, labeled Maryland and SDSU

University of Maryland Extension, Jerry Brust, “Problems with Pollination in High Tunnel Tomatoes,” updated 22 October 2024: tomatoes are “self-pollinated at the rate of around 96% of the time.” Flowers are complete; “tomato pollen is heavy and sticky and needs to be jostled loose.” “Without pollination the pedicle turns yellow, the flower dies and then drops. Tomato flowers must be pollinated within 50 hours of forming or they will abort. Pollination usually occurs between 10 a.m. and 4 p.m.” Temperature: flowers drop when daytime temperatures are “above 88°–90°F” or nighttime “above 70°F”; early in the season, “low nighttime temperatures below 55°F can interfere with the growth of pollen tubes or cause the pollen to become sterile,” and “fruit will not set until nighttime temperatures are above 55°F for at least two consecutive nights.” High tunnels may lack the wind that jostles flowers in the field. Bumblebees use “sonication or buzz pollination”; a visit leaves a mark growers can use to confirm activity. “Studies have shown that just 1-2 visits by bumblebees to tomato flowers will result in greater than 80% fruit set vs no visits which result in approximately 30% fruit set.” Brust also reports a personal high-tunnel air-blower trial in which per-plant yields went “from 18 lbs to 28 lbs” and marketable yield rose “by 35-50%.” Those yield figures are this author’s trial in his tunnels, not an Aevani or Intermountain guarantee, and not a bumblebee result.

SDSU Extension, Geoffrey Njue, “Pollinating Tomatoes in High Tunnels,” updated 3 April 2019: field wind is “not generally” enough inside a tunnel. Manual or mechanical methods “should be applied during the middle of the day when the humidity in the high tunnel is 60-80%.” “Low night temperatures (below 55° F) inhibits pollen development and high day temperatures (90°F and above) leads to flower abortion. High humidity conditions (above 80% relative humidity) cause pollen grains to stick together.” This page prints both sources’ temperature and humidity numbers and does not invent a tighter house band.

## Replace the missing wind

Penn State Extension, “High Tunnel Production,” updated 13 March 2023: “For crops that are planted very early, be ready to remove row covers, thermal blankets, or low tunnels to facilitate pollination. Most growers using high tunnels find that purchased colonies of bumblebees increase both the quality and quantity of fruit. These should be ordered several weeks in advance of required delivery time.”

SDSU describes the manual and mechanical options. Tapping support strings or wires every other day, or at least three days per week, can be sufficient for small growers. Battery-operated vibrators go on the stem of the flower cluster, not the flower, every other day on sunny days. Household (not gasoline) leaf blowers can be directed at clusters “7-8 feet down the row,” at least three times a week — faster than a vibrator, “however the quality and yield of fruit is lower (smaller fruit size and weight)” because pollen may be blown off. “Gasoline-powered air blowers should not be used in the greenhouse to avoid ethylene toxicity.”

Bumble bees, in SDSU’s account, “are active from sunrise to sunset and at low temperatures (as low as 41°F).” “Using bumble bees in small high tunnels can lead to over pollination and injury to the flowers because even the smallest hive has more bees than a small high tunnel can sustain.” Work with the supplier on hive size. This page does not invent a bees-per-square-foot figure that the opened pages do not print, and it does not name a hive brand.

University of Kentucky ID-235 (revised October 2018), already used in the [greenhouse IPM](/guides/greenhouse-ipm) guide: close and remove bumblebee colonies before using pollinator-toxic insecticides, storing bees in a shady location for a day before returning them. Only greenhouse-and-crop labeled pesticides. This page is not a spray chart.

Ventilation and heat around the same structure are on the [ventilation](/guides/greenhouse-ventilation-heat) and [high tunnels](/guides/high-tunnels-hoop-houses) guides. Early covers also appear in the [season-extension](/guides/season-extension-covers-frames) guide; they still have to come off for bloom.

## Common mistakes

Leaving row covers on through bloom is the first (Penn State). Blaming “bad seed” when nights were below 55 °F or days were around 90 °F is the second. Gasoline blowers in a closed house is the third (SDSU: ethylene). A hive that is too big for a small tunnel is the fourth (SDSU). Spraying a pollinator-toxic insecticide with the bees still in the house is the fifth (Kentucky). Treating Brust’s personal yield trial as a universal result is the sixth.

## What to do with the usual questions

**Tomato flowers are falling off, or do I need bumblebees?** Official first checks: temperature and humidity, then whether the flowers are being jostled. Bumblebees are the method Penn State says most tunnel growers use; SDSU warns that a small tunnel can have too many bees.

**Can I just shake the plants?** SDSU describes tapping support strings or wires every other day, or a vibrator on the cluster stem, as methods that can be enough for small growers.

**Does lettuce need this?** No. This page is for fruiting crops that require pollination or a pollen jostle.
