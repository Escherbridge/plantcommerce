**Container gardening: pot size, media, and watering**

# Container gardening: pot size, media, and watering

A container crop fails in predictable, official ways: a pot with no drainage, garden soil in a bucket, a tomato in a pot that is too small, and a summer watering habit that does not keep up. Official advice is almost boring, and it would have saved most failed patio tomatoes: holes in the bottom, a soilless mix, a large enough pot, and water that comes out the holes on a hot day. This page is about those land-grant sizes and media rules, not about a decorative planter.

## Hero figures

Penn State Extension (updated 30 March 2026) and Ohio State University Extension (HYG-1647-2000) do not print identical diameters. Both are official, and this page prints both rather than inventing a compromise number. The gallon column and the diameter column in the Ohio sheet should also not be mashed into one rule.

- **Penn State (2026):** “A single tomato plant will need at least a 20-inch-wide pot, while peppers and eggplants can thrive in a 14-inch pot. Smaller pots are good for herbs and greens. For healthy root growth, all pots should be at least as tall as they are wide.”
- **Ohio State (2000):** “Tomatoes produce best in containers of at least 20" or 22" diameter, peppers grow best in 16" diameter.” The same sheet also states volumes: peppers, chard, and dwarf tomatoes, a soil volume of 1–2 gallons per plant; full-sized tomato plants and cucumbers, 4–5 gallons per plant. A capacity table includes: 8" pot, 1½ gallons; 10" pot, 2½ gallons; 12" pot, 3½ gallons; 14" pot, 4½ gallons; 16" pot, 5½ gallons; 20" pot, 6½ gallons. Lettuce is listed as an 8"-wide, 6–8" deep example.

University of Maryland Extension’s “Types of Containers for Growing Vegetables” is a supporting land-grant page on container types. This guide does not add sizes from that page beyond what Penn State and Ohio already printed clearly.

## Drainage, media, and water

Penn State names four operating points: drainage, size, potting mix, and water and fertilizer. “No matter what you’re growing in your container, it should have drainage holes at the bottom.” A saucer that holds the pot in standing water undoes the holes. Fill with a soilless mix — “do not use straight garden soil.” Soilless mixes (sphagnum moss, composted bark, perlite, vermiculite, or coconut coir) drain while holding water and nutrients and are “free from weed seeds and plant pathogens.” Finished compost may be added, “up to 50%.”

Ohio agrees: “Do not use 100% garden soil (often clay soil), which is too heavy, dense, and compact.” That is the same mistake as filling a [raised bed](/guides/raised-beds) with unamended yard clay, just in a smaller box. [Understanding soil health](/guides/understanding-soil-health) is a related note, not a substitute for a potting mix.

Water “at least once a day during hot summer months,” Penn State says, until water drains from the bottom; media should be moist, never waterlogged. Ohio: containers “may need daily watering on a very hot day, and some may need water twice a day.” There is no weekly schedule in these sheets. Watering the surface and never soaking until drainage is how a large pot stays dry in the middle. Add a slow-release fertilizer at planting and a soluble fertilizer every two to four weeks (Penn State). Plastic pots dry out more slowly than terracotta; heavier pots help keep tomatoes from blowing over. Ohio notes that porous clay containers should be brought inside for winter to prevent cracking; most non-porous containers may be left out.

## What not to grow in a pot

Penn State: tomatoes and peppers need at least 6 hours of sun; lettuces and kale prefer some shade. Choose dwarf, bush, or determinate types when possible; sweet corn, watermelon, winter squash, and zucchini are “better-suited for in-ground gardening.” Expecting a patio zucchini to behave like a raised-bed plant is a crop-choice mistake, not a watering one. If you have ground, the [raised beds](/guides/raised-beds) guide is the better fit. When the question is the whole parcel, [plan the land on PlantGeo](https://plantgeo.aevani.com).

## Common mistakes

No drainage holes, or a saucer that holds the pot in standing water, is the first. Garden soil in a bucket is the second, and both fact sheets already forbid it. A tomato in a too-small pot is the third; show both official tables instead of picking a marketing diameter. Watering the surface and never soaking until drainage is the fourth. Expecting a patio zucchini to behave like a raised-bed plant is the fifth.

## What to do with the usual questions

**How do I start container vegetables?** Start with drainage, a soilless mix (not garden soil), and a pot sized to the crop. That is the whole official beginner list.

**How big a pot for tomatoes or peppers?** Penn State: at least 20 inches wide for one tomato, 14 inches for peppers or eggplant. Ohio: 20–22 inches for tomatoes, 16 inches for peppers, plus the gallon table. Cite both.

**How often do I water?** Penn State: at least once a day in hot summer months, until water drains. Ohio: daily, and some pots twice on very hot days.

## An illustrative scenario

This is a worked example, not a customer and not a testimonial. A full-size tomato sits in a 10-inch decorative pot of yard soil, watered lightly once a day in July. The pot has no real drainage, the mix is heavy, and the plant was asked to live in less root room than either official table describes.
