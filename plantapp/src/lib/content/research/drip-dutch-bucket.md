**Drip and Dutch-bucket hydroponics**

# Drip and Dutch-bucket hydroponics

A tomato is not a lettuce that needed more time in the pipe. Official Florida production moved long-season fruiting crops out of narrow NFT channels and into drip-fed media: Dutch buckets, lay-flat bags, and rockwool slabs. The number that tells you whether the bag is quietly salting up is the leachate EC, not only the tank reading. This page is a delivery guide, not a bucket brand, and Florida recipes are labeled Florida.

Florida's hydroponic systems guide, originally published in January 2010, describes a common perlite Dutch-bucket system as plastic, two-gallon buckets with a two-inch reservoir at the bottom. “Drip irrigation supplies water and nutrients, which overflow the reservoir and are collected to drain or recycled through a PVC pipe.” A related layout is drip-irrigated, perlite-filled, lay-flat plastic bags. In non-recirculating media systems “such as the lay-flat bag, Dutch bucket or rockwool systems, which do not recirculate the nutrients… root infections remain localized.” That is the official contrast with a shared NFT or raft loop. Rockwool culture “was the most common hydroponic production system in Florida during the 1990s”; later Florida practice, as the same handbook notes, moved among perlite bags and buckets.

Florida's water-and-nutrient guide, originally published in December 2015 and reviewed in April 2025, by Robert C. Hochmuth, Natalie B. Parkell, Wanda L. Laughlin, and Sean C. Rider: “Most Florida irrigation water has an EC of 0.5 or less (dS m⁻¹) and after the nutrient solutions are made and delivered to the crop, the final EC is usually in the range of 1.5 to 3.5 (dS m⁻¹). In most cases, the EC of a young crop starts in the lower range and is increased gradually as the crop matures.” “In a soilless media system, the EC of the leachate from the slabs or bags should not vary more than 1.0 EC units (dS m⁻¹) above or below the EC of the applied nutr[ient solution].” “If the leachate EC is excessive (3–4 units higher) then flushing the media should be done with water. However, after flushing in this manner, the farmer should resume the properly adjusted (likely lower EC) nutrient solution concentration, and not keep giving only water for an extended period of time.” “It should be noted that the recommended nutrient solutions for crops grown in southern climates are much lower in nutrient concentration than those suggested in cooler, northern climates such as Canada. Florida growers should not follow northern nutrient solution recipes or use the leachate guidelines from those cooler, northern areas.” Those water and recipe figures are Florida’s, not Treasure Valley chemistry, and a northern recipe is not automatically local either.

University of Arizona Controlled Environment Agriculture Center, the fertigation chapter of the University of Arizona greenhouse manual, states operating bands for that greenhouse tomato crop and climate: “pH should be ~6. EC is typically ~2.0 - 3.5 (but may vary ~1.2 - 4).” “For mature tomatoes: Drip EC = 2.5 – 3.5 mS/cm (drain ~1 unit higher).” Those are CEAC tomato-drip figures, not a universal vegetable law.

Florida's tomato nutrient guide, revised in December 2024, gives a final solution pH of 5.8–6.2 for greenhouse tomatoes and tells growers to test well water for pH, carbonates, sulfur, magnesium, calcium, and iron. Most Florida well water in that publication is pH greater than 6.5 — again, Florida, not Idaho. The [source-water testing](/guides/source-water-testing) guide is where that test list belongs in more detail.

Florida's greenhouse vegetable production guide, revised in September 2018 is the cautionary tale already told in the [nutrient film technique](/guides/nutrient-film-technique) guide: essentially all of the north-Florida PVC-tomato NFT growers in that account “have since switched to bag or other soilless media culture.” The pairing of fruiting crops with media, and leafy crops with films and rafts, is spelled out in [choosing crops for a hydroponic system](/guides/hydroponic-crop-choice).


## How the container is supposed to work

A Dutch bucket, in Florida's hydroponic systems guide’s picture, is a two-gallon perlite container with a small bottom reservoir, drip in, and overflow out. The overflow can drain to waste or be recycled. A lay-flat bag and a rockwool slab are siblings of the same idea: a volume of inert media, a drip line, and a leachate you can catch and measure. This page does not name a commercial tower or bucket brand. The method is the container, the drip, and the number on the runoff.

Non-recirculating media is the publication’s example of keeping a root infection in one container. Recirculating overflow is also described. This page does not invent a sanitation protocol beyond that contrast.

Daily operating numbers still sit on the [EC and pH](/guides/hydroponic-ec-ph-nutrients) page. Do not flatten the CEAC tomato bands or UF’s 5.8–6.2 tomato pH against Oklahoma State's crop table, which is printed on the crop-choice sibling. [Choosing a first hydroponic system](/guides/choosing-hydroponic-system) is the comparison menu this layout belongs in.

## Common mistakes

Growing long-season tomatoes in 4-inch NFT PVC because it worked for lettuce is the first; UF already documented the switch. Never measuring leachate, only the tank, is the second. Flushing with water and then staying on water is the third (Florida's water-and-nutrient guide). Copying a Canadian or desert tomato recipe into Florida water, or a Florida recipe into Intermountain well water, without a source-water test is the fourth. Naming a bucket brand as if it were the method is the fifth.

## What to do with the usual questions

**What is Dutch-bucket hydroponics, and how does it differ from NFT?** Official picture: a drip-fed media container with a small reservoir and a drain, used for fruiting crops that need more root volume than a shallow film. NFT is a thin, moving film in a sloped channel, which official hints treat as a better fit for short-lived, small-rooted crops.

**What EC should the runoff be?** UF Florida's water-and-nutrient guide: stay within 1.0 dS/m of the incoming solution. If leachate is 3–4 units higher, flush, then go back to a correctly adjusted (likely lower) feed. Do not stay on plain water.

**Should I recirculate the drain?** Florida's hydroponic systems guide describes both drain-to-waste and recycled overflow. Non-recirculating media is the publication’s example of keeping a root infection localized.

A bag that was never leachate-tested, EC climbing, then a week of plain water “to fix it,” which is the thing Florida's water-and-nutrient guide says not to do.
