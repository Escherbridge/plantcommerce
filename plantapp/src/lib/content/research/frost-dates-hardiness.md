**Frost dates, hardiness zones, and planting calendars**

# Frost dates, hardiness zones, and planting calendars

A half-zone is a winter-extreme average, not a permission slip for tomatoes. Official pages already split the tools: the USDA map is about winter survival of perennials; a planting calendar is about average frost dates and soil warmth, and those dates are local. This is a map-and-calendar explainer, not a frost-date table invented for Idaho.

## What the 2023 map is, labeled USDA

The USDA Agricultural Research Service 2023 Plant Hardiness Zone Map, and the official “How to Use the Maps” explainer: the map “is based on the average annual extreme minimum winter temperature, displayed as 10-degree F zones ranging from zone 1 (coldest) to zone 13 (warmest). Each zone is divided into half zones designated as ‘a’ and ‘b’… 5-degree F increments.” “Zones in this edition of the USDA PHZM are based on 1991-2020 weather data. This does not represent the coldest it has ever been or ever will be in an area, but it simply is the average lowest winter temperatures for a given location for this 30-year span (1991-2020).” Growing a plant “at the extreme range of the coldest zone where they are adapted means that they could experience a year with a rare, extreme cold snap.” The how-to page also notes microclimates: heat islands, frost pockets in small hills and valleys, a sheltered or exposed yard. Use the official interactive map rather than inventing a zone for a reader’s address.

USDA ARS news release, “USDA Unveils Updated Plant Hardiness Zone Map” (2023): the 2023 map “incorporates data from 13,412 weather stations compared to the 7,983 that were used for the 2012 map.” Compared with 2012, “about half of the country shifted to the next warmer half zone, and the other half of the country remained in the same half zone.” Map developers “cautioned against attributing temperature updates made to some zones as reliable and accurate indicators of global climate change (which is usually based on trends in overall average temperatures recorded over long time periods).” That is USDA’s own caution. The map is a perennial-survival guide, not a proof about global climate, and this page does not write a climate-policy paragraph around the update.

Boise-area half-zones and climate normals already live on the [Intermountain hydroponics](/guides/hydroponics-intermountain) guide: ZIP 83702 is zone 7a (0 to 5°F average annual extreme minimum) and ZIP 83716 is zone 6b (−5 to 0°F), on the 2023 map, plus official NOAA normals for Boise Air Terminal. This page does not invent a new Idaho frost table, and it does not invent University of Idaho bulletin numbers that could not be opened as a current, clean official page for this brief.

## Planting dates, labeled USU, and kept local

Utah State University Extension, “Utah Vegetable Planting Date Calculator,” using *Suggested Vegetable Planting Dates for Utah* (Olsen, revised 2025): “Dates are approximate and based on average frost dates for each location. Adjust for your garden’s microclimate and elevation. Fall planting dates are general estimates and are not location-specific.” “Utah planting dates can vary by two or three months because of elevation and local climate.” “Utah does not have one statewide last frost date.” Cool-season vegetables may go in before the last frost; “warm-season vegetables such as tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers and squash should generally be planted after frost danger has passed and the soil has warmed.” “Treat the date as a practical target rather than a guarantee.” This is the official Intermountain planting-calendar analog opened for this brief. It is Utah, not Idaho. This page does not copy a Utah city date and label it Boise.

USU also says dates are approximate; delay if a freeze is forecast, the soil is cold or wet, or your microclimate is harsher. Cool-season crops are the ones official planting guides put before last frost; tomatoes generally after. Season-extension tools that can move a date, without inventing a new one, are on the [row covers, low tunnels, and cold frames](/guides/season-extension-covers-frames) guide. Shutting a structure down, or keeping it from cooking on a March afternoon, is on the [winterizing](/guides/winterizing-growing-systems) page.

## Common mistakes

Using a hardiness zone as a last-frost date is the first. Treating the zone as the coldest it can get is the second (USDA: it is an average extreme). Copying a Utah city date onto a different elevation or across the state line is the third. Inventing University of Idaho frost tables is the fourth. Writing a climate-politics paragraph around the 2023 map update is the fifth.

## What to do with the usual questions

**What growing zone is Boise, and when do I plant tomatoes?** Official split: look up the 2023 USDA map for perennial hardiness; use local average frost dates and soil warmth for annual vegetables. Boise-area half-zones and climate normals already live on the Intermountain hydroponics guide. This page will not invent a new Idaho frost table.

**Did my zone change in 2023?** About half the country moved one warmer half-zone on the 2023 map, USDA says; the other half did not. Look up the address. That still is not a planting date. USDA cautions against reading the shift as a reliable indicator of global climate change.

**Can I plant two weeks before the average last frost?** USU: dates are approximate; delay if a freeze is forecast, the soil is cold or wet, or your microclimate is harsher. Cool-season crops are the ones official planting guides put before last frost; tomatoes generally after.
