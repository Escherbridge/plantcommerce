**Nutrient film technique: slope, flow, and channel length**

# Nutrient film technique: slope, flow, and channel length

NFT is a thin, moving film of solution in a sloped channel. Official guides already disagree slightly on how much slope is enough, and they agree completely that a stopped pump or a standing puddle is the failure. A system with almost no water in it cannot afford to stop. This page is a channel-design guide, not a gutter kit, and it does not flatten the official slope figures into one invented house number.

## Hero figures, labeled by source

- **Oregon State University Extension EM 9457 (May 2025, peer-reviewed):** NFT is a thin, continuous film of nutrient-rich solution bathing the roots. “Maintaining a minimum 2% slope in horizontal NFT channels ensures proper nutrient film flow.” “Backups for power and pumps are crucial. Power loss or pump failure can lead to immediate plant wilting due to the cessation of the nutrient film.”
- **UF/IFAS HS785 / CV263 (revised September 2018):** true NFT has plants in a shallow, plastic-lined trough with solution flowing continuously or in short on/off cycles. Roots spread across “4–12-inch flat-bottomed channels” and are “bathed in a thin film of flowing, oxygenated nutrient solution.” “Some systems call for a 1-inch drop for every 100 inches of channel length” (a 1% slope). “This is probably not enough slope for the NFT PVC tubes. An inch rise for every 20–30 inches might be more appropriate” (about 3–5%).
- **FAO Technical Paper 589 (2014), aquaponic NFT pipes:** water flow “for each grow pipe should be no greater than 1–2 litres/min,” and “a slope of about 1 cm/m of pipe length is needed” (1%). Leafy crops in that section may use 7.5 cm diameter pipes; larger fruiting vegetables “11 cm diameter grow pipes are needed”; for mixed small-scale plantings, 11 cm. Those are FAO aquaponic-pipe figures, not a hydroponic lettuce-gutter law, and they do not erase OSU’s 2% minimum or UF’s steeper PVC note.

University of Kentucky CCD-CP-63 (2025) contrasts oxygenation: NFT has no air pump — “continuous thin film provides oxygen” — unlike [deep water culture](/guides/deep-water-culture), which needs air stones and a 6–8 ppm dissolved-oxygen target. A thin film that has become a puddle is no longer that system.

## What belongs in a channel

OSU: channels “are best suited for short-lived or small-rooted crops such as herbs and leafy greens.” UF: “Lettuce is particularly adapted to NFT because it is a short-term crop and less subject to damage from root rot.” Transplants are “usually started in rockwool or foam cubes.” In true NFT, “the plastic liner is wrapped around the stems to prevent algae growth in the channel.” Light on the film is also a topic in the [troubleshooting](/guides/hydroponic-troubleshooting-root-rot) guide.

UF’s cautionary tale is tomatoes in 4-inch PVC, a popular hobby and 1980s north-Florida commercial idea: “The PVC tube is extremely confining to the root system, making continual bathing of the root system impossible because it stops water flow and causes the root systems to flood.” Even with pulsed irrigation, “many growers still have problems with oxygen availability to the roots. Pythium root rot then can attack the roots.” “Essentially all PVC tomato growers have since switched to bag or other soilless media culture.” [Choosing a first hydroponic system](/guides/choosing-hydroponic-system) already notes that official fruiting-crop work often uses drip in media. UF/IFAS HS405 is the supporting reminder that in a recirculating water-culture system a root pathogen can spread.

Shallow channels, OSU says, “are sensitive to ambient temperature changes”; mitigate by “increasing slope, shortening channel lengths and using larger stock tanks.” This page does not invent a maximum length in feet or meters; OSU says “shortening” without a number. Vertical NFT arrangements “can significantly increase crop density” but “adding more vertical levels will increase shading,” which “may require using low-light crops or providing supplemental lighting.” A later vertical-growing guide is queued. Nutrient targets still belong on the [EC and pH](/guides/hydroponic-ec-ph-nutrients) page. If the loop includes fish, FAO’s pipe figures also appear in [aquaponic system types](/guides/aquaponic-system-types).

## Common mistakes

A “film” that is actually a standing puddle is the first; official advice is slope and flow, not a deep stream. No backup for the pump is the second (OSU: wilting is immediate). Tomatoes in 4-inch PVC because it “worked for lettuce” is the third (UF: growers left that system). Flattening OSU’s 2%, UF’s 1% / steeper-PVC note, and FAO’s 1 cm/m into one invented house slope is the fourth. Endless channels that heat and starve the last plants are the fifth; OSU’s mitigation is to shorten them.

## What to do with the usual questions

**What does an NFT setup include?** A sloped channel, a thin moving film, a reservoir and pump, a covered root zone, and a crop with a small root system. Official slope figures above, labeled by source.

**What happens if the pump stops?** OSU: immediate wilting. That is the design risk.

**Can I grow tomatoes in NFT?** UF describes attempts in narrow PVC, oxygen problems, Pythium, and a general move to media culture. Lettuce is the crop the recent official hints call “particularly adapted.”
