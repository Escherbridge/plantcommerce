**Daily light integral for indoor and greenhouse greens**

# Daily light integral for indoor and greenhouse greens

A day of light is a total, not a moment, and a wattage sticker is not a measurement. Official greenhouse pages already define daily light integral as the day’s total of photosynthetic light, and they already published lettuce targets. Those targets are not identical, and this page prints them labeled rather than averaging them into a house number. This is a measurement guide, not a bulb catalog, and it does not invent a watt-per-square-foot chart or a tomato DLI table.

## What DLI is, labeled Purdue

Purdue Extension HO-238-W, “Measuring Daily Light Integral in a Greenhouse,” Ariana P. Torres and Roberto G. Lopez. A publication year was not printed on the extracted pages opened for this brief, and none is invented here. Photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) is light from 400 to 700 nm. Instantaneous horticultural measurements are in µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹. Foot-candles and lux are instantaneous and “photometric” — “focused on people and not appropriate for indicating plant photosynthesis.” “Daily light integral (DLI) is the amount of PAR received each day as a function of light intensity (instantaneous light: μmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹) and duration (day). It is expressed as moles of light (mol) per square meter (m⁻²) per day (d⁻¹), or: mol·m⁻²·d⁻¹ (moles per day).” DLI inside a greenhouse depends on structure, season, cloud cover, day length, shading, and obstructions such as hanging baskets. Supplemental lighting can raise the day’s total; shade curtains and whitewash can reduce it. The publication’s Figure 1 is a monthly outdoor DLI map after Korczynski, Logan, and Faust 2002. That map’s year is 2002. It is not a 2023 Intermountain measurement. Virginia Tech, below, cites Faust and Logan (2018) for later monthly U.S. DLI maps; prefer that later citation over treating Purdue’s 2002 figure as the current map.

## Lettuce targets, labeled and not averaged

University of Kentucky CCD-CP-63 (2025), hydroponic lettuce: “Lettuce is a cool season crop and the air temperature for hydroponic lettuce production should not exceed 75 °F (24 °C) during the day and 65 °F (18 °C) at night, with an optimal daily light integral (DLI) of 12-17 mol/m²/day.” “The minimum DLI requirement for lettuce ranges from approximately 6.5 to 9.7 mol/m²/day. However, growing lettuce under low light and temperature conditions will extend the time from planting to harvest.” Figure 1 in that bulletin plots ambient and estimated inside-greenhouse DLI in Kentucky from Mesonet stations, 2021–2024, “assuming a 50% light transmission rate,” and notes that “for many Kentucky farms, winter lettuce production is possible with little to no supplemental lighting.” That winter remark is a Kentucky figure with a 50% transmission assumption, not a Boise greenhouse forecast. Use a light meter or quantum sensor; “lower-cost light sensors (usually measured in W/m² or lux) can be used with spectral conversion factors.” Seedling instantaneous targets already used in the [seed-starting](/guides/soilless-seed-starting) guide — about 50 µmol/m²/s to start germination, then 200–300 µmol/m²/s as seedlings develop — are instantaneous readings, not DLI.

Virginia Cooperative Extension SPES-462NP (2023), Latimer, Vallotton, Sperry, Mullins, and Scoggins, *Hydroponic Production of Edible Crops: Management Basics*: DLI is “the total moles of light accumulated in a 24-hour period.” “The optimum DLI for lettuces is about 17 mol/m²/day.” A clear summer day in Virginia is “about 40 to 44 mol/m²/day” and winter “15 to 20 mol/m²/day” (cited there to the American Floral Endowment); “the greenhouse structure and glazing can reduce the light received by the crop by 30% to 50%.” “Therefore, without supplemental lighting, a lettuce crop that finishes in 35 days in the summer will require twice that time to finish in the winter.” “The rule of thumb is that a 1% increase in light level will provide a 1% increase in yield of leafy green biomass.” That is Virginia Tech’s printed rule of thumb, not a measured Intermountain result, and this page does not turn it into a lighting-ROI calculator or a guarantee.

UKY’s optimal band is 12–17 mol/m²/day, with a minimum of about 6.5–9.7 and a slower crop at the low end. Virginia Tech’s optimum is “about 17.” Those are not the same instruction. This page does not average them, and it does not treat the minimum as “optimal.” Structures cut light by a large fraction (UKY’s figure assumes 50%; Virginia Tech says 30–50%). Intermountain winter DLI is not printed on these pages and is not invented here. The climate a closed loop still has to live in is on the [Intermountain hydroponics](/guides/hydroponics-intermountain) guide; air and heat around a structure are on [greenhouse ventilation and heat](/guides/greenhouse-ventilation-heat).

Cornell CEA’s *Hydroponic Lettuce Handbook* (2013) already specifies instantaneous PAR and photoperiod in its lettuce nursery recipe (Day 0: 50 µmol/m²/s, 24-hour photoperiod; Day 1 onward in that recipe: 250 µmol/m²/s, still 24 hours in the opened pages). Those figures belong on the seed-starting sibling and are a commercial lettuce recipe, not a general houseplant law.

## Common mistakes

Buying lights by watts or foot-candles is the first. Averaging UKY’s 12–17 and Virginia Tech’s “about 17” into a fake house target, or treating the minimum 6.5–9.7 as “optimal,” is the second. Copying Kentucky’s winter “little supplemental light” into a Treasure Valley garage is the third. Turning Virginia Tech’s 1% / 1% rule of thumb into a guarantee is the fourth. Inventing a photoperiod or watt-per-square-foot chart is the fifth.

## What to do with the usual questions

**What is DLI, and how much light do indoor greens need?** Official answer: measure the day’s PAR total. Official lettuce bands above, labeled by source.

**Can I use a cheap lux meter?** Kentucky says lower-cost sensors in W/m² or lux can be used with spectral conversion factors. Purdue: lux and foot-candles are not, by themselves, the right photosynthetic unit.

**Do I need lights in a winter greenhouse?** It depends on the day’s DLI inside the structure, not on the month’s name. Kentucky and Virginia Tech give regional estimates; they are not an Idaho measurement.
