**Troubleshooting hydroponic root rot, algae, and pH drift**

# Troubleshooting hydroponic root rot, algae, and pH drift

Brown roots are usually a water problem, not a mystery disease.

The official lettuce and controlled-environment guides already tie soft, brown roots and a drifting reservoir to warm solution, low dissolved oxygen, dirty starting material, and unsanitized gear. They do not start with a bottle of miracle treatment. The internet often wants a dose. The useful first tools are a thermometer, a dissolved-oxygen reading if the roots sit in water, and the same EC and pH meters the [nutrient guide](/guides/hydroponic-ec-ph-nutrients) already assumes you own.

These are lettuce and CEA set points, not a universal law for every crop and every room.

Cornell's 2013 lettuce handbook says lettuce “will grow satisfactorily at a DO level of at least 4 ppm.” If no oxygen is added to the pond, DO “will drop to nearly 0 ppm,” and the absence of oxygen “will stop the process of respiration and seriously damage and kill the plant.” The level is usually maintained at 8 ppm, in a 7–10 ppm band, with “no advantage to 20.” A set-point table lists 7 mg/L or ppm, and “crop failure if less than 3 ppm.” A figure in Cornell's handbook notes that DO should be greater than 4 ppm to prevent growth inhibition; visible signs of stress may be observed at 3 ppm.

University of Kentucky's 2025 lettuce systems guide, on deep-water culture, says “Need air pump and air stones, 6–8 ppm dissolved oxygen.” A dissolved-oxygen meter “is also highly valuable for ensuring oxygen availability in the root zone, which supports healthy root development and reduces the risk of root diseases.”

Neil Mattson’s 2018 leafy-green teaching notes: a typical nutrient-solution temperature of 65–75°F to delay bolting and “reduce Pythium infection,” with dissolved oxygen of 7 ppm and crop failure below 3 ppm. Those are conference teaching notes from a named Cornell CEA faculty member, not a trial report, and they are not a climate-wide promise.


## What you are looking at

University of Kentucky describes Pythium root rot (*Pythium* spp., *Phytopythium* spp., *Globosporangium* spp.) as caused by “a group of soilborne, fungus-like pathogens.” Symptoms: plants wilt, with marginal leaf scorch or yellowing of outer leaves that becomes more severe as root rot progresses; “roots become soft, mushy, and grayish-brown, and the cortex” is affected. Management listed there includes: do not introduce diseased plugs into production systems, and remove diseased plants as soon as they appear. Handheld infrared thermometers are recommended to monitor leaf and nutrient-solution temperatures.

In UKY’s system comparison, DWC oxygenation needs an air pump and air stones at 6–8 ppm; NFT relies on a continuous thin film; drip and media systems rely on air space in the substrate. Florida's hydroponic systems guide, originally published in January 2010, is the design reminder: in a recirculating water-culture system a root pathogen can spread, which is one reason some fruiting-crop growers use non-recirculating media systems where “root infections remain localized.” That is a layout implication, not a Florida industry history. [Choosing a first hydroponic system](/guides/choosing-hydroponic-system) is the comparison page if the build itself is still the question.

Cornell also notes that for sufficiently small systems, an air pump and aquarium air stone can add air, “but the dissolved oxygen level achieved will not be as high as can be achieved with pure oxygen,” which is the commercial pond method in that handbook. An air stone helps. It is not a magic substitute for a measured DO floor.

## A short diagnostic order

**Look at the roots and the solution temperature.** Soft, mushy, grayish-brown roots and wilting outer leaves are the UKY picture. Mattson’s teaching range for solution temperature is typically 65–75°F. Warm water also holds less oxygen, which is why a warm, stagnant reservoir fails two ways at once. This page does not invent a single cutoff beyond what Mattson and Cornell printed.

**Measure dissolved oxygen if the system is water-culture.** UKY and Cornell both treat a DO meter as a real tool, not a luxury, in solution-based systems. Satisfactory lettuce growth in Cornell’s pond is at or above 4 ppm; stress may be visible around 3 ppm; usual operation is 7–10 ppm.

**Measure EC and pH.** Drift is a clue. High EC can injure roots, and chasing pH while the water is warm and stagnant treats the meter as the whole problem. Cornell’s lettuce recipe is pH 5.6–6 and EC 1,150–1,250 µS/cm above source water. The [EC and pH guide](/guides/hydroponic-ec-ph-nutrients) has the official tables and the rule to set EC first.

**Ask whether the plugs or the last crop’s gear were clean.** UKY’s first management line is not to introduce diseased plugs. Replanting into unsanitized channels or rafts is how the last crop’s problem becomes the next crop’s starting condition.

**In NFT, ask whether the “film” has become a puddle.** That question shows up in real beginner threads, and official NFT detail belongs in a later NFT guide. A standing channel is not the thin film UKY says supplies the oxygen.

## Algae and light

Official pages opened for this brief do not give an algae-bleach recipe, and this page does not invent a peroxide dose. Light on the solution grows algae. Algae are a symptom of an uncovered reservoir or channel, and they compete for oxygen and nutrients. Block the light. If you are deciding whether an air stone is required in a bucket system, UKY already lists an air pump and air stones and a 6–8 ppm target for DWC.

Public grower threads are evidence of the questions, not sources of treatment recipes. A 29 September 2025 r/Hydroponics thread asked, in a first indoor basil system with algae and root rot, how to stop algae coming back, how often to change the water, whether an airstone is required, and whether to cool the water. A 5 July 2023 thread, “First hydro, brown roots,” discussed pH spikes, channel temperature, and NFT water depth. Unverified home remedies from those threads, including peroxide dosing, are not official guidance and are not repeated here as recipes. This page also will not diagnose a stranger’s channel from a photo.

## What to do with a sick plant

UKY: remove diseased plants promptly, and do not introduce bad plugs. UF: understand whether your system recirculates (pathogens travel) or not. There is no official one-shot “treatment” in the pages opened for this guide, and this page does not promise a cure rate. Treating brown roots as a nutrient deficiency and turning the EC up is the opposite of helpful if salts are already high. Recirculating from a dirty tank into a clean one is how UF’s spread problem gets built by hand.

Heat around the structure still matters. [Hydroponics in the Intermountain West](/guides/hydroponics-intermountain) is about the climate a closed loop has to live in, including summer heat that can push solution temperature around.

## Common mistakes

Treating brown roots as a nutrient deficiency and raising EC is the first. Chasing pH while the water is warm and stagnant is the second. Recirculating from a dirty tank into a clean one is the third. Replanting into the same unsanitized channels or rafts is the fourth. Offering or repeating unofficial peroxide, bleach, or “beneficial in a bottle” recipes that were not opened as official sources is the fifth, and this page will not do it. Taking Cornell’s pond set points or Mattson’s 65–75°F band as a climate-wide promise is the last.

## What to do with the usual questions

**How do I treat or prevent Pythium in hydroponics?** Official starting points: keep the solution oxygenated (Cornell, UKY), keep it from running too warm (Mattson teaching notes), start with clean plugs, and pull infected plants (UKY). There is no official one-shot treatment in the pages opened here.

**How do I keep algae from coming back? Should I cool the water? Is an airstone required?** Keep light off the solution. Solution temperature and dissolved oxygen are linked. In DWC, UKY lists an air pump and air stones and a 6–8 ppm target. Cornell says a small-system air stone helps but will not match the pure-oxygen dosing used in some commercial ponds.

## A situation like this

A warm indoor bucket, no air stone, lid off so light hits the solution, and seedlings from a reused tray. The roots turn brown, algae come back after a water change, and the missing ingredient was not a treatment bottle.
