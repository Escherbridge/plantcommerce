**Deep water culture: aeration and dissolved oxygen**

# Deep water culture: aeration and dissolved oxygen

Deep water culture is simple in layout and strict about dissolved oxygen. The hardware is a raft or a net pot over a reservoir. The operating constraint is whether the roots can breathe, and official lettuce guides have already printed the floors. A stagnant, warm reservoir is the usual way the simplicity fails. This page is about those oxygen numbers, not about a branded air-pump kit.

## Hero figures

These are lettuce and CEA figures, not a universal law for every crop.

- **University of Kentucky CCD-CP-63 (2025), lettuce comparison table:** DWC oxygenation is “Need air pump and air stones, 6–8 ppm dissolved oxygen.” NFT, by contrast, has no air pump — the thin film supplies oxygen — and drip or media systems rely on air space in the substrate.
- **Cornell CEA lettuce handbook (2013):** lettuce “will grow satisfactorily at a DO level of at least 4 ppm.” If no oxygen is added to the pond, DO “will drop to nearly 0 ppm,” and the absence of oxygen “will stop the process of respiration and seriously damage and kill the plant.” Usual operating level: 8 ppm, in a 7–10 ppm band, with “no advantage to 20.” A set-point table lists 7 mg/L or ppm, and “crop failure if less than 3 ppm.” Figure 18: greater than 4 ppm to prevent growth inhibition; visible stress may be observed at 3 ppm.
- **Neil Mattson’s 2018 leafy-green teaching notes:** dissolved oxygen of 7 ppm, crop failure below 3 ppm, and a typical nutrient-solution temperature of 65–75°F to reduce Pythium infection. Those are conference teaching notes, not a trial report.

## How the system is supposed to work

UF/IFAS HS405 (original January 2010) describes floating-raft, deep-water systems as Styrofoam rafts with holes, floated on nutrient-rich water, a method that later became popular in Florida for short-cycle crops and “was not even reported in 1991” in that survey. That is the official picture of raft DWC, not a national adoption figure. Short-cycle greens are the published fit.

The plant is supported above the solution. The roots hang in aerated nutrient water. UKY’s short version is the clearest: a reservoir, an air pump, air stones, and a 6–8 ppm dissolved-oxygen target, plus calibrated meters. UKY calls a dissolved-oxygen meter “highly valuable” in solution-based systems “to reduce the risk of root diseases,” and recommends watching nutrient-solution temperature. pH and EC still have to be managed; Cornell’s lettuce recipe on this system is pH 5.6–6 and EC 1,150–1,250 µS/cm above source water. The [EC and pH guide](/guides/hydroponic-ec-ph-nutrients) has the official tables. [Choosing a first hydroponic system](/guides/choosing-hydroponic-system) is the comparison if you are still deciding among NFT, DWC, Kratky, ebb and flow, and drip.

For small systems, Cornell says an air pump and aquarium air stone can be used, “but the dissolved oxygen level achieved will not be as high as can be achieved with pure oxygen,” which is the commercial pond method in that handbook. An air stone is a real tool. It is not a requirement to install pure-oxygen dosing on a home tote, and bubbles in the tank are not the same thing as a measured 6–8 ppm.

## How it fails

Warm, stagnant water is the first failure. Mattson’s teaching band of 65–75°F is there in part to reduce Pythium infection; warm water also holds less oxygen. Air stones that are clogged, unplugged, or simply too small for the volume are the second. Light leaking into the reservoir grows algae, which then compete for oxygen and nutrients; the [troubleshooting guide](/guides/hydroponic-troubleshooting-root-rot) is the checklist once roots are already soft and brown. A power cut to the air pump is the quiet version of “if no oxygen is added, DO will drop to nearly 0 ppm.”

[Kratky](/guides/kratky-method) is not aerated DWC. It is a related-looking suspended-pot system that relies on an air gap as the water level drops, and it has a published crop-water limit. An unaerated bucket is not “DWC without a pump.” Loading a first DWC with a long-season fruiting crop is also a mismatch: the raft literature UF is describing is short-cycle work. Climate around the reservoir still matters; [hydroponics in the Intermountain West](/guides/hydroponics-intermountain) covers heat and source water without repeating those figures here.

This page does not invent bucket volumes, stone counts, or a “change the water every X days” schedule. Official pages opened for this brief do not print those house rules.

## Common mistakes

Calling an unaerated Kratky jar “DWC” is the first. Assuming bubbles equal a measured 6–8 ppm is the second. Leaving the reservoir where light and heat can work on it is the third. Using Cornell’s commercial-pond “pure oxygen” line as a home-system requirement is the fourth; Cornell itself says small systems may use an air stone, with a lower ceiling. Loading a first DWC with a long-season fruiting crop the raft literature is not describing is the fifth.

## What to do with the usual questions

**What does a deep water culture setup actually include?** Officially: a reservoir, a support for the plant, aerated nutrient solution, and meters. UKY’s table is the clearest short version (air pump, air stones, 6–8 ppm).

**What dissolved oxygen should I maintain?** Cornell and UKY numbers above, labeled as lettuce/CEA figures, not a universal crop law. Satisfactory lettuce growth in Cornell’s pond is at or above 4 ppm; usual operation is 7–10 ppm; stress may be visible around 3 ppm. UKY’s DWC target is 6–8 ppm.

**Is an air stone required?** For DWC as UKY describes it, an air pump and air stones are in the system definition. Kratky is the published no-pump alternative, with its own limits.
