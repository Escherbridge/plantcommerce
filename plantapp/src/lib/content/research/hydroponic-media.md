**Soilless hydroponic media: perlite, rockwool, and coir**

# Soilless hydroponic media: perlite, rockwool, and coir

A bag of mix is not a system. Official hydroponic pages already treat media as a physical choice — support, drainage, disposal — and they name the usual substrates. They also print one official water-holding ranking, from a Florida greenhouse bulletin, and they do not publish a coco-versus-rockwool scorecard. This page uses those figures and does not invent others. It is a media-choice guide, not a brand shelf.

Florida's hydroponic systems guide, originally published in January 2010, is the production-systems menu for media. Perlite appears in Dutch buckets and drip-irrigated lay-flat bags; other systems use “various media-filled nursery pots or upright bags”; rockwool slabs are a separate culture. “Rockwool is an inert, fibrous material produced from” melted mineral, “extruded as fine threads and pressed into loosely woven sheets. Use of rockwool declined in favor of perlite primarily because of the greater cost of materials and the difficulty of disposal; in both of these areas, perlite has an advantage.” That is a Florida industry observation from that publication, not a ban on rockwool and not a universal cost table. NFT is a no-media water-culture method; raft systems float plants on boards, not in a bag. Non-recirculating media (lay-flat bag, Dutch bucket, rockwool) is the publication’s example of keeping a root infection localized, already used in the [drip and Dutch-bucket](/guides/drip-dutch-bucket) guide.

Florida's water-and-nutrient guide, by Hochmuth, Parkell, Laughlin, and Rider, originally published in December 2015 and reviewed in April 2025, ties media choice to irrigation management and prints official water-holding comparisons. “Different media have varying water-holding capacities, and as a result, each grower’s production system may require different irrigation schedules. For instance, coconut fiber holds at least twice the amount of water as a similar volume of standard horticultural-grade perlite or composted pine bark. In general, the relative water-holding capacity of standard grades of common media is coconut fiber, pine bark, and perlite, from greatest holding capacity to least. Therefore, every grower should conduct the soilless media water-holding capacity test.” The test: weigh dried media in the actual container, saturate until drainage stops, re-weigh, and “divide the weight of the water by 8.34 (one gallon of water weighs 8.34 pounds).” “For reference, a three-gallon upright bag of fine coconut fiber holds approximately one gallon of water. In contrast, horticultural-grade perlite, composted pine bark, and coarse coconut fiber will hold much less water per volume of media, about half of the amount of water held by fine coconut fiber.” “It is expected that a mature fruiting tomato or cucumber plant will use 2–2.5 quarts of water per day during peak harvest” — that is this Florida greenhouse bulletin’s expectation, not a Boise trial. “Plants growing in high moisture-holding media such as fine coconut fiber may need irrigation 6–8 times daily, whereas plants growing in a low moisture-holding media like horticultural-grade perlite may need a dozen or more shorter events daily.” “Night-time irrigation events are not typically needed in hydroponic vegetable production because transpiration is low at night.” Rockwool slabs are mentioned as shipped “dried,” but this publication does not print a coco-versus-rockwool water-holding number, and this page does not invent one. The leachate rule from the drip guide still applies: leachate EC should stay within ±1.0 dS/m of the incoming solution; if it runs 3–4 units high, flush, then resume a correctly adjusted feed.

Penn State Extension’s hydroponics nutrition article (updated 2 February 2026) includes aggregate culture — sand, gravel, coir — under hydroponics defined as growing without soil. “Soilless mix” and “hydroponics” are not opposites. Oklahoma State's hydroponics guide lists common substrates: “perlite, vermiculite, rock wool, expanded clay, coconut coir, wooden fiber or a mixture of substrates like perlite and coconut coir.” That is a list of what is used, not a performance ranking.

FAO's 2014 aquaponics manual recommends volcanic gravel, 8–20 mm, washed of dust, as the popular medium for aquaponic media beds, “where available.” That belongs as a pointer to [aquaponic system types](/guides/aquaponic-system-types), labeled aquaponic, not as a hydroponic fertilizer-bench default.

Cornell and Kentucky lettuce nurseries, on the [seed-starting](/guides/soilless-seed-starting) sibling, use 1-inch rockwool or bonded plugs because those plugs keep their shape in water. Disposal and cost, from UF’s Florida note, still apply at production scale.


## What the roots can sit in

Perlite bags and Dutch buckets; rockwool slabs; pots and upright bags; coir, vermiculite, expanded clay, and wood fiber (Oklahoma State’s list); bonded plugs and rockwool cubes in the nursery. You do not need a bag in NFT or raft culture. You do in Dutch buckets, bags, slabs, and most ebb-and-flow benches. [Choosing a first hydroponic system](/guides/choosing-hydroponic-system) and the [NFT](/guides/nutrient-film-technique) guide are the layout siblings.

The physical jobs of a medium are support, drainage, a place for a dripper, and — in aquaponic media beds — filtration. Water-holding differs, and the grower is told to test the actual bag, not to treat the three-gallon fine-coco reference as a law for every container. Night events are typically not needed, in that Florida bulletin. This page does not invent a minutes-per-hour schedule beyond those official ranges, and it does not name a branded clay or coco block as if it were the official method. It also does not invent a recycling program; UF’s Florida snapshot says rockwool disposal is harder than perlite, and stops there.

## Common mistakes

Inventing water-holding percentages the bulletin does not print, especially a coco-versus-rockwool comparison, is the first. Treating Florida's water-and-nutrient guide’s three-gallon-bag reference as a universal law for every container is the second. Putting a peat-heavy potting mix into a thin-film or raft system it will fall apart in is the third (Kentucky: bonded plugs for water culture). Never measuring leachate because “the bag looks wet” is the fourth. Treating FAO volcanic gravel as a hydroponic tomato-slab recommendation is the fifth.

## What to do with the usual questions

**What is the best hydroponic medium, and can I use coco?** Official pages list perlite, rockwool, coir, and others as used substrates. UF Florida's hydroponic systems guide says rockwool lost ground to perlite in Florida on cost and disposal. Florida's water-and-nutrient guide ranks coconut fiber ahead of pine bark and perlite for water-holding and tells the grower to test their own container. Penn State includes coir in the soilless and hydroponic definition. No opened page crowns a single winner.

**Do I need media at all?** Not in NFT or raft culture. You do in Dutch buckets, bags, slabs, and most ebb-and-flow benches.

**How often do I water a bag?** Florida's water-and-nutrient guide: fine coconut fiber may need 6–8 events a day; horticultural-grade perlite may need a dozen or more shorter events; a mature fruiting tomato or cucumber in that Florida bulletin may use 2–2.5 quarts per day at peak harvest. Night events are typically not needed. Test the bag and check leachate.
