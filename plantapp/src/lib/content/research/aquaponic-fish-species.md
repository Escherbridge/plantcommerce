# Choosing aquaponic fish by species and temperature

FAO already published the temperature and water-quality windows for the usual aquaponic species. Tilapia is not a winter fish in an unheated Intermountain tank, and a tropical fish is not a personality trait. It is a thermostat. This page stays on species and climate. Stocking density and the compromise table already live in [small-scale aquaponics](/guides/small-scale-aquaponics). This is not a live-fish offer.

## The frame, briefly

FAO the species table (general tolerances) sets a warm-water column and a tighter cold-water column. Warm-water fish: 22–32 °C, pH 6–8.5, ammonia <3 mg/litre, nitrite <1, dissolved oxygen 4–6 mg/litre. Cold-water fish: 10–18 °C, ammonia <1, nitrite <0.1, DO 6–8. The aquaponic compromise already documented in the stocking guide (the water-quality table) is 18–30 °C, pH 6–7, ammonia <1, nitrite <1, nitrate 5–150, DO >5. FAO’s own comment: “The general temperature range is 18–30 °C, and should be managed in regard to the target fish or plant species cultivated… It is important to choose appropriate pairings of fish and plant species that match well with the environmental conditions.” Trout, as the paper later shows, sits outside that general 18–30 °C window. The 20 kg of fish per 1,000 litres of water cap stays on the stocking page; this page does not rewrite that math.

Unheated structures, ventilation, and heat already live in [hydroponics in the Intermountain West](/guides/hydroponics-intermountain). Use that page as climate context, not as a fish-survival table it does not contain. This page does not invent a local tank temperature for a Boise winter. It prints FAO’s thresholds.

## Species cards, labeled FAO 2014

**Tilapia (*Oreochromis* spp.).** “Although tilapias briefly tolerate water temperatures extremes of 14 and 36 °C, they do not feed or grow below 17 °C, and they die below 12 °C. The ideal range is 27–30 °C.” “Therefore, in temperate climates, tilapias may not be appropriate for winter seasons unless the water is heated. An alternate method for cool climates is to grow multiple species throughout the year, rearing tilapias during the warmest seasons and switching to carp or trout during the winter.” In ideal conditions FAO describes growth from a 50 g fingerling to 500 g in about six months — the paper’s ideal-condition figure, not a local guarantee. “Exotic (i.e. non-native) species should never be released into local bodies of water.” Check local rules before importing any species.

**Carp (*Cyprinus carpio* and related species).** “Carp can survive at temperatures as low as 4 °C and as high as 34 °C making them an ideal selection for aquaponics in both temperate and tropical regions. Best growth rates are obtained when temperatures are between 25 °C and 30 °C.” “Growth rates dramatically decrease with temperatures below 12 °C.” A wide survival band is not the same thing as best growth.

**Catfish.** “Like tilapia, catfish grow best in warm water and prefer a temperature of 26 °C; but in the case of African catfish growth stops below 20–22 °C.” FAO also notes a high tolerance to low dissolved oxygen and high ammonia relative to other species discussed, which is not a reason to ignore water quality.

**Rainbow trout (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*).** “All trout require colder water than the other species previously mentioned, preferring 10–18 °C with an optimum temperature of 15 °C. Trout are ideal for aquaponics in Nordic or temperate climate regions, especially in winter. Growth rates significantly decrease as temperatures increase above 21 °C; above this temperature trout may not be able to properly utilize DO even if available.” They “require a high protein diet compared with carp and tilapia,” which FAO says “allows for more cultivable areas of leafy vegetables while maintaining a balanced aquaponic unit.” “Overall, trout require better water quality than tilapia or carp, particularly with regard to DO and ammonia,” plus backups for air and water pumps. the species table’s tighter cold-water ammonia and nitrite columns belong in the same breath.

**Largemouth bass (*Micropterus salmoides*).** “Growth will only cease at less than 10 °C or more than 36 °C; they will stop feeding at temperatures less than 10 °C. The optimal growth temperatures are in the range of 24–30 °C.” “For a good FCR the optimal DO is above 4 mg/litre.” FAO calls them a possible year-round choice “particularly for those who cannot change species between cold and warm seasons.”

Layout and start-up sit on [aquaponic system types](/guides/aquaponic-system-types) and [cycling](/guides/aquaponic-biofilter-cycling). UVI’s nearly-5-mt tilapia figure on the layout page is a research-system yield, not a species recommendation for a backyard tank.

## Common mistakes

Choosing tilapia because “everyone uses tilapia,” then discovering 12 °C, is the first. Treating the 18–30 °C compromise as if it included trout is the second. Rewriting stocking density on a species page is the third, and this page will not do it. Releasing leftover fish into a local waterway is the fourth (FAO: never). Ignoring trout’s tighter ammonia, nitrite, and DO needs is the fifth.

## What to do with the usual questions

**What is the best fish for aquaponics? Can you raise trout? What temperature do tilapia need?** FAO’s answer is climate-first: tilapia in reliably warm water, trout in cold water, carp with a wide survival band but growth that still likes 25–30 °C, and a seasonal switch where winters are real.

**Will tilapia survive a Boise winter outdoors?** FAO: they die below 12 °C and stop feeding below 17 °C unless the water is heated. This page does not invent a local tank temperature.

**Can I keep mixed species in one tank?** FAO describes seasonal switching and some polyculture attempts (for example bass with tilapia). This page does not invent a mixed-tank recipe that the paper does not give.
