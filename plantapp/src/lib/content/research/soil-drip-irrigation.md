**Drip irrigation for raised beds and high tunnels**

# Drip irrigation for raised beds and high tunnels

Plastic hides the soil. Official raised-bed and high-tunnel pages already treat drip or soaker lines as the default, and they already say you schedule from soil moisture under the plastic — not from a sprinkler habit, and not from a guess. This is a soil-and-tunnel drip page, not a redo of [drip and Dutch-bucket hydroponics](/guides/drip-dutch-bucket). It does not invent a Boise evapotranspiration calendar, and Minnesota nitrate-credit arithmetic stays labeled Minnesota.

## Raised beds, labeled USU

Utah State University Extension, “Raised Bed Gardening,” February 2012, by Rick Heflebower, Katie Wagner, JayDee Gunnell, and JoDeane Condrat: “A raised bed garden must be irrigated. Drip irrigation is a simple and efficient method that is perfectly suited for this method.” Raised-bed soils “warm more quickly in the spring, allowing for earlier planting. However, higher soil temperatures will increase evaporative moisture loss, requiring more frequent irrigation during the hot summer months.” “Irrigation of raised beds is most efficient when using some form of ‘drip’ or ‘trickle’ irrigation. Irrigation can be accomplished with a simple soaker hose wound throughout the box or a more sophisticated system using drip tubing controlled by a timer.” “Sprinkler irrigation is an additional option; however, sprinkler heads are not as water efficient and they water outside the planting box.” Ensure a water source and that water “freely drains through the soil, away from the plant rooting zone.” The publication points to a USU fact sheet, “Designing a Basic PVC Home Garden Drip Irrigation System,” for installation. That is a pointer, not a manual reopened here. Width, depth, and fill still belong on the [raised beds](/guides/raised-beds) guide.

## Tunnels, labeled Penn State

Penn State Extension, “High Tunnel Production,” updated 13 March 2023: horticultural crops in tunnels “are produced more efficiently on raised beds with plastic mulch and drip irrigation.” Drip tape is “generally placed two inches deep and can be set either in the center or just off center, depending on the crop.” Tomatoes: tape on one side of the bed, a single row down the middle. Peppers: tape in the middle, two rows set diagonally. Greens and cut flowers “may require a second line of drip tape due to higher planting densities.” After transplanting through plastic, “monitor soil moisture levels underneath the plastic mulch with a tensiometer or irrometer, and maintain soil moisture levels by using drip irrigation.” Rain exclusion plus drip and mulch “greatly reduces weeds and diseases.” Additional nutrients can be “injected through the drip irrigation tape” based on tissue sampling. That is a commercial-tunnel practice; this page does not invent a backyard fertilizer recipe. The structure itself is on the [high tunnels](/guides/high-tunnels-hoop-houses) guide.

## The water itself, labeled UMN

University of Minnesota Extension, “Soil health and nutrient management in high tunnels,” reviewed 2026: test irrigation water “at least once” for pH and alkalinity, and “test for nitrate in your water regularly.” An example arithmetic for a nitrate credit is printed: N credit in lb/acre per inch of water = nitrate-N in ppm × 0.225 × inches applied. That is UMN’s formula and example, not Treasure Valley well chemistry, and it is not copied onto an untested Intermountain well. Salts can build up in a tunnel “from over-application of inputs, use of irrigation water with high alkalinity, and fertigation.” Soils with more organic matter and clay “allow moisture to move away from the drip irrigation emitters.” Fertigation is common in tunnel drip systems; nitrogen and potassium are the nutrients most often injected. Penn State and UMN describe injection through drip in commercial tunnels, based on tests. This page does not invent a backyard stock-tank recipe. pH, alkalinity, and source-water testing in more detail live on the [source-water](/guides/source-water-testing) guide.

Utah State University Extension, “Irrigation System Maintenance,” July 2021, already used in the [winterizing](/guides/winterizing-growing-systems) guide, is the official drain or professional blow-out pointer at the end of the season. This page does not invent a mid-season maintenance checklist beyond what that page and the production guides say.

## Common mistakes

Sprinkling a narrow box and watering the path is the first (USU). Guessing at moisture on top of the plastic is the second (Penn State: measure underneath). One drip line for a dense greens bed that the production guide says may need two is the third. Copying a Minnesota nitrate credit onto an untested Intermountain well is the fourth. Never draining the system before a freeze is the fifth (USU maintenance).

## What to do with the usual questions

**How often should I water a raised bed or a high-tunnel tomato?** Official leaning: drip or soaker in the bed; schedule from soil moisture, especially under plastic, not from a fixed clock these pages do not print.

**Where does the tape go?** Penn State’s crop examples: tomatoes, tape to one side; peppers, tape in the middle; dense plantings may need a second line; generally two inches deep in that tunnel system.

**Can I fertigate?** Penn State and UMN describe injection through drip in commercial tunnels, based on tests. This page does not invent a backyard recipe.
