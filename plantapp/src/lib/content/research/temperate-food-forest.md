**Temperate food forests: layers, guilds, and how to start**

# Temperate food forests: layers, guilds, and how to start

A food forest, in the official pages opened here, is a layered planting, not a yield forecast we do not have. South Dakota State University Extension already defined it: a diverse planting of edible and beneficial plants that mimic the layers found in a forest, starting as small as one tree and its helpers. NC State describes how a lawn can become a multi-story planting over a few years. The official record is short — a two-page SDSU publication and an NC State handbook appendix — and this page stays inside that line. It does not invent pounds per acre, a stocking rate, or an Intermountain species list.

Livestock and trees are a different guide: [temperate silvopasture](/guides/temperate-silvopasture). This page is plants only.

## Layers and guilds, labeled by source

South Dakota State University Extension the Midwestern food-forest publication2, *Edible Landscaping & Food Forests*, Robin Buterbaugh, © 2026 South Dakota Board of Regents: “A food forest is a diverse planting of edible and beneficial plants that mimic the layers found in a forest.” “Food forests contain plants at multiple levels with specific characteristics that support each other to function as a whole system.” Layers named in the publication’s text and graphics include a canopy (large fruit or nut trees), a low or dwarf tree layer, a shrub layer (berries), an herb layer, a ground-cover or surface-soil layer, a root layer, and a vertical or vine layer. The graphic also marks insectaries, nitrogen-fixers, medicinals, windbreak, and shade. “A food forest can be many acres, several acres, or even a single tree in your yard with the supporting plants around it.” “Guilds are a type of polyculture that includes a tree and other plants grown in layers that help each other in some way.” Tips: “Observe and learn from the local ecosystem about what plants work best.” “Start small, work in phases so you don’t get overwhelmed. Begin with one fruit tree guild and expand from there if you have the time and space.”

The publication includes example plant lists. If those lists are used at all, they are SDSU’s examples, not an Intermountain recommendation. Check local extension and invasive-species guidance before planting a species from a South Dakota list in another climate. This page does not reprint those lists as a shopping catalog.

NC State Extension Gardener Handbook, Appendix G, “Permaculture Design,” by Abbey Piner (Urban Horticulturalist, Horticultural Science). The handbook page lists a publication date of 1 February 2022 (AG-831). No later revision date is given, and none is invented here. A fruit-tree guild, in that appendix, plants several other plants around the base of a tree, “chosen and placed intentionally for its ability to support the tree and surrounding plants in some way. A guild might include a productive fruit tree, a mulching plant, a plant whose roots slow erosion and hold soil, a plant that attracts beneficial insects, and a plant to block the wind.” Figure the Midwestern woodland-garden note is “an apple tree guild with pollinator attracting crown vetch (*Securigera varia*), a dynamic accumulator comfrey (*Symphytum* spp.), and human attracting strawberries planted below.” That figure is NCSU’s illustrated example, not an Aevani plant list, and not a planting recommendation. Check local invasive-species lists before planting anything from an example guild, including crown vetch. This page does not add invasiveness claims the appendix does not make.

## How to start, without flattening two sequences

SDSU’s step-by-step guide:

1. Site assessment and planning — climate, soil type, sunlight, water; goals and size; plan the layers (canopy, understory, shrub, herbaceous, ground cover, root); draw a layout with spacing.
2. Site preparation — designate paths; “prepare the area for planting by sheet mulching with cardboard or newspaper, organic matter, and wood chips or other material”; arrange water access.
3. Planting — “plant the trees first”; then layer shrubs, herbs, and groundcovers; “follow plant spacing recommendations”; fence if needed to protect from animals.
4. Maintenance — monitor and adjust; mulch (wood chips, grass clippings, straw, or mulch plants) and weed; prune fruit trees and remove dead or diseased plants; water as needed; harvest.

The how-to for cardboard and layers is on the [no-dig and sheet-mulching](/guides/no-dig-sheet-mulch) guide, not reprinted here. [Raised beds](/guides/raised-beds) remain a different, framed method.

NCSU’s succession note is a second official sequence, not a rewrite of SDSU’s. The appendix describes working “to facilitate succession in a space, transitioning a grassy lawn into a multi-story food forest in a matter of a few years through tactics such as sheet mulching, cover cropping, and planting in several different stories. Start with a ground floor, and slowly add taller and larger plants as the initial story matures.” SDSU says plant the trees first. NCSU says start at the ground and add height as it matures. This page prints both and does not flatten them into one invented rule.

What the sources do not give us: no pounds per acre, no “feeds a family,” no Intermountain cultivar list. SDSU says observe the local ecosystem and start small. That is the honest end of the official record opened here.

## Common mistakes

Treating a two-page extension publication as a production manual, or inventing yields to fill the gap, is the first. Copying SDSU or NCSU plant examples into another climate without a local check is the second. Flattening SDSU’s “trees first” and NCSU’s ground-to-height succession into one invented rule is the third. Skipping sheet mulch and paths is the fourth (SDSU step 2). Turning this into a livestock page is the fifth.

## What to do with the usual questions

**What is a food forest, and what are the layers?** Official definition: SDSU’s layered planting of edible and beneficial plants that mimic a forest, down to a single tree and its supporting plants. Official start: assess, sheet-mulch, plant trees first, or, in NCSU’s succession language, build stories over a few years.

**How big does it have to be?** SDSU: many acres, several acres, or a single tree in a yard.

**What should I plant in Idaho?** These two pages do not answer that. SDSU says observe the local ecosystem and use local knowledge. This page does not invent an Intermountain list.

## A situation like this

A weekend “food forest” of random saplings, no paths, no sheet mulch, no water plan, and a later weedy thicket. SDSU already wrote the order.
