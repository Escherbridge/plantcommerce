**Hydroponic nutrient disorders: check pH and EC first**

# Hydroponic nutrient disorders: check pH and EC first

A deficiency name is often a skipped meter reading. Official nutrition pages already describe the common deficiency patterns, and they also already say pH and EC problems can look like a missing nutrient. This page starts with a meter, not with a color chart we did not open, and not with a single element from a forum post. Official patterns come second, quoted, and they still are not a lab test.

## Meter first

Penn State Extension’s hydroponics nutrition article (updated 2 February 2026, Elsa Sánchez) states that plants need 17 essential elements and gives a general hydroponic vegetable pH range of 5.0–7.0, reminding readers that the pH scale is logarithmic. How to take the daily reading belongs on the [EC and pH](/guides/hydroponic-ec-ph-nutrients) page; what to test in the well or municipal supply belongs on [source-water testing](/guides/source-water-testing). Those two pages come before any “add calcium” advice.

UF/IFAS HS787 / CV265 (George Hochmuth; original December 1990, revised June 2025) tells growers to “maintain pH at 5.5 to 6.5” in the media discussion and not to over-lime peat in trough culture. The form of nitrogen also moves pH: “As NH₄ is absorbed, the plant releases H⁺ ions… and therefore, the pH drops. As NO₃ is absorbed, the pH increases.”

Penn State Extension, “Hydroponic Nutrient Solution Recipes and Formulations” (updated 18 March 2024, Elsa Sánchez and Francesco Di Gioia), explains that a recipe has to be adjusted for nutrients already in the source water. The opened example is of the form 210 ppm potassium in the target minus 30 ppm already in the water, so 180 ppm is added. That is the principle, labeled Penn State 2024, not a full recipe table and not a fertilizer brand.

## Mobile versus immobile, labeled UF (revised June 2025)

The patterns below are UF’s greenhouse-vegetable descriptions, not a homemade photo key and not a backyard tank law to apply without a lab.

Older leaves first: nitrogen, potassium, and magnesium. New growth first: calcium, iron, and often sulfur.

- **Nitrogen:** “Nitrogen is a very mobile element in the plant and deficiency symptoms therefore show up first on the lower leaves. Symptoms consist of a general yellowing (chlorosis) of the leaves.” Excess nitrogen: plants “usually are lush and tender with larger and darker-green leaves.” Normal leaves in that publication are given as 2.0–5.0% nitrogen on a dry-weight basis. Those percentages are tissue figures from this greenhouse-vegetable publication, not a tank-ppm target.
- **Potassium:** “Deficiency symptoms for K show up first on lower leaves as a marginal flecking or mottling. Prolonged deficiency results in necrosis along the leaf margins and the plants can become slightly wilted. Deficient plant leaves usually contain less than 1.5% K.”
- **Calcium:** “Calcium is immobile in the plant; therefore, deficiency symptoms show up first on new growth.” “Deficiencies of Ca cause necrosis of the new leaves or lead to curled, contorted growth. Examples of this are tip burn of lettuce and cole crops. Blossom-end-rot of tomato also is a calcium-deficiency related disorder.”
- **Sulfur:** Nitrogen and sulfur deficiencies “appear similar,” with the publication distinguishing location: nitrogen deficiency on the lower leaves, sulfur deficiency on the upper leaves. “S deficiency is not very common in greenhouse vegetable crops.”
- **Iron:** “Iron is not mobile in plants and symptoms appear on the new leaves first. Symptoms consist of interveinal chlorosis that may progress to a bleaching and necrosis.”
- **Magnesium:** “Unlike Ca, Mg is mobile in the plant and deficiencies appear first on the lower leaves,” as “an interveinal chlorosis which can lead to necrosis.”

University of Kentucky CCD-CP-63 (2025) treats lettuce tipburn as a calcium-plus-airflow problem, not only a fertilizer miss. The bulletin links tipburn to calcium movement and air movement, and prints an airflow band of 0.7–2.2 mph (0.3–1.0 m/s) in that lettuce context. A still house can look like a missing element. This page does not invent a fan model or a spray rate.

Crop-specific solution ranges still sit in [choosing crops for a hydroponic system](/guides/hydroponic-crop-choice). If the roots are already brown, start with the [troubleshooting](/guides/hydroponic-troubleshooting-root-rot) guide rather than a bottle of one element.

## Common mistakes

Naming a deficiency from a leaf photo without reading pH and EC is the first. Treating lettuce tipburn as only a fertilizer miss is the second (Kentucky: air movement). Adding one element that pushes another out of range, without a source-water-adjusted recipe, is the third. Inventing or scraping a color-diagnostic chart that the official pages did not publish as a stand-alone key is the fourth. Applying UF dry-weight tissue ranges as if they were a tank-ppm target is the fifth.

## What to do with the usual questions

**Why are the leaves yellow, and is it a nutrient deficiency?** Official first step: meter the solution, and the source water. Then match old-leaf versus new-leaf patterns from UF: general yellowing of older leaves suggests nitrogen; marginal flecking of older leaves suggests potassium; new-leaf necrosis, lettuce tipburn, or tomato blossom-end rot points at calcium (and, for tipburn, airflow).

**Is it nitrogen or sulfur?** UF: they can look similar; nitrogen shows on lower leaves, sulfur on upper leaves, and sulfur deficiency is “not very common” in that greenhouse-vegetable context.

**Can I just add calcium?** Not as the first move. Check pH, EC, and airflow (Kentucky, for lettuce). Adjust the recipe for source-water calcium already present (Penn State).
