**Hydroponic nutrients, EC, and pH**

# Hydroponic nutrients, EC, and pH

The two numbers that actually run a soilless system.

Soil buffers a mistake. A reservoir does not. Oklahoma State University Extension is blunt about that absence, and it treats electrical conductivity and pH as the daily operating numbers: check EC first, get it in range, then check pH, at the same time each day. Official crop tables exist, and they do not all print the same lettuce pH or the same tomato EC. This page is about those meter readings, not about a nutrient brand.

## Hero figures

- **Check EC first, then pH, daily, at the same time of day** (Oklahoma Cooperative Extension, HLA-6722, October 2016). Nutrient solutions for soilless culture should have a pH between 5 and 6 (usually 5.5), so the root environment stays between 6 and 6.5, “the pH range at which nutrients are most readily available to plants.”
- **UF/IFAS, hydroponic tomatoes:** final solution pH 5.8 to 6.2 (HS796 / CV216, revised December 2024). After nutrients are added, “the final EC is usually in the range of 1.5 to 3.5 (dS m⁻¹),” starting lower on a young crop (HS1274, reviewed April 2025). 1 dS/m = 1 mS/cm.
- **Cornell CEA lettuce handbook (2013):** solution pH 5.6–6, and EC 1,150–1,250 µS/cm above the source water (1.15–1.25 mS/cm above source). Always subtract source-water EC when you read the reservoir.

## What the meters are actually telling you

Total salt concentration, pH, alkalinity, and the nutrient concentration ratio are the four main characteristics OSU tells you to manage. Electrical conductivity is a stand-in for the salt load, not a complete recipe. Excessively high nutrient levels, in OSU’s wording, “induce osmotic stress, ion toxicity and nutrient imbalance”; excessively low values are “mostly accompanied by nutrient deficiencies and decreasing plant growth.”

Penn State Extension, updated 2 February 2026, defines hydroponics here as growing plants without soil and gives a wide general band: “in general, the optimal pH range for growing vegetables hydroponically is 5.0 to 7.0.” That is a general vegetable range, not a crop recipe. The same article reminds you that the pH scale is logarithmic: a value of 7 is 10 times higher than 6 and 100 times higher than 5. Plants need 17 essential nutrients; carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen come from air and water, and the rest come from the solution or the media.

## Test the source water before you mix

OSU, UF/IFAS, and Cornell all start with the water you already have. A source-water test should include pH, EC, and alkalinity, not a single pH dip. OSU Table 1 lists acceptable values in ppm: sodium under 50, calcium under 150, magnesium bicarbonate under 50, chloride under 140, and sulfate under 100. High-alkalinity water (over 75 ppm) “will cause the pH to increase in the nutrient solutions,” so pH has to be checked more often. UF asks for a water sample analyzed for pH, carbonates, S, Mg, Ca, and Fe, and notes that most well water in Florida has pH greater than 6.5, with most Florida irrigation water at an EC of 0.5 or less. Those Florida well statements are Florida well statements. They are not Intermountain well chemistry, and this page does not invent a Treasure Valley number to stand in for a lab report.

## Published crop tables differ, and that is useful

OSU Table 2, “Optimum range of electrical conductivity (EC) and pH values for hydroponic crops,” is one official table, not a law of nature. As printed:

- Lettuce: 1.2 to 1.8 mS/cm, pH 6.0 to 7.0
- Basil: 1.0 to 1.6 mS/cm, pH 5.5 to 6.0
- Spinach: 1.8 to 2.3 mS/cm, pH 6.0 to 7.0
- Cucumber: 1.7 to 2.0 mS/cm, pH 5.0 to 5.5
- Peppers: 0.8 to 1.8 mS/cm, pH 5.5 to 6.0
- Tomato: 2.0 to 4.0 mS/cm, pH 6.0 to 6.5

OSU’s lettuce pH column (6.0–7.0) sits higher than the same fact sheet’s general solution target (pH 5–6, usually 5.5). UF’s tomato target is 5.8–6.2. Cornell’s lettuce recipe is 5.6–6. Flattening those lines into one “ideal pH” is how you stop reading the table. A leafy-green recipe is also not automatically a fruiting-crop recipe: OSU’s lettuce and tomato EC bands do not overlap much, and UF says tomato demand changes through the season (small amounts early, then more as fruit load increases). The Florida tomato program is described as a suitable base when planning cucumbers and peppers, with the caveat that cucumbers need more nitrogen early because they grow faster.

In soilless media, UF HS1274 says leachate EC “should not vary more than 1.0 EC units (dS m⁻¹) above or below the EC of the applied nutrient solution.” If leachate EC is 1 unit high, irrigation or incoming concentration may be off. If it is 3–4 units high, the media should be flushed with water, then the grower should resume a correctly adjusted (likely lower) nutrient solution, and “not keep giving only water for an extended period of time.”

## A working order of operations

**Calibrate the meters.** You need a calibrated pH meter and an EC meter, not a color kit you guess from. OSU’s procedure is the unglamorous one: calibrate, stir, wait for a stable reading, adjust slowly, and clean and store the probe.

**Mix and set EC, then adjust pH.** That order is the OSU rule, and it is the one people skip when they chase a pH reading in an unfinished recipe. Once EC is in the crop’s published band, or in the lower part of UF’s 1.5–3.5 dS/m delivered range on a young crop, then bring pH into the crop’s published band.

**Top up and interpret drift, without inventing a house schedule for reservoir changes.** Official pages opened for this guide do not give a universal daily or weekly dump-and-replace calendar, so this page does not invent one. EC rising often means water is leaving faster than salts; EC falling often means the opposite. If pH keeps climbing, alkalinity is a usual suspect (OSU’s >75 ppm flag), and a single source-water pH reading is not a complete test.

## Common mistakes

Adjusting pH before EC is in range is the first one, because it treats the symptom of an unfinished mix as the whole problem. The second is treating OSU’s lettuce column, OSU’s general solution target, UF’s tomato 5.8–6.2, and Cornell’s 5.6–6 as if they were one number. Ignoring alkalinity is the third. Using “the plant looks fine” in place of a calibrated meter is the fourth; official fact sheets assume daily readings. The fifth is flushing media with water for a long stretch after a high leachate EC, instead of the short flush-then-resume sequence UF describes. This page also will not quote unsourced “90% less water” or “30–50% faster growth” lines.

## What to do with the usual questions

**What pH should I maintain?** Official ranges vary by crop and source. Penn State’s general vegetable range is 5.0–7.0. OSU’s general solution target is pH 5–6 (usually 5.5). UF tomatoes: 5.8–6.2. Cornell lettuce (2013): 5.6–6. Use the crop and the source, not a forum average.

**What EC should I maintain?** OSU Table 2 is one official crop table (lettuce 1.2–1.8 mS/cm; tomato 2.0–4.0). UF describes delivered greenhouse solutions usually at 1.5–3.5 dS/m, starting lower on young plants. Cornell lettuce: 1.15–1.25 mS/cm above source water. Subtract the source-water EC.
