**Three guides · climate first, catalogue later**

# Same climate. No invented figures.

These pages are growing-system guides, not product pages. They take official climate, water-quality, and practice documents and apply them to hydroponics, small-scale aquaponics, and temperate silvopasture in an Intermountain / high-desert setting. The figures are other people’s, with the links left on. The Aevani catalogue is a preview, and nothing in it is for sale.

The shared pattern is simple: a closed loop, a tank, or a paddock still has to live in the weather and the management it actually has. At Boise Air Terminal, official 1991–2020 normals include 11.51 inches of annual precipitation and 54.8 days at or above 90°F (NOAA). Central Boise is USDA zone 7a; some foothills ZIPs are 6b (2023 Plant Hardiness Zone Map). Those numbers are cited in full on the child pages. This index does not invent a market size, a harvest count, or a water-savings percentage to sit above them.

## The first three

- **[Hydroponics in the Intermountain West](/guides/hydroponics-intermountain)** — 11.51 inches of official annual precipitation, and 54.8 days at or above 90°F (NOAA, Boise Air Terminal). What constrains a closed system is heat, winter nights, source-water salts, and cooling water, not a missing kit.
- **[Small-scale aquaponics](/guides/small-scale-aquaponics)** — FAO’s compromise is pH 6–7, ammonia close to zero, and a 20 kg / 1,000 L stocking cap for the small-scale units in the 2014 technical paper. The compromise in the tank is the whole design.
- **[Temperate silvopasture](/guides/temperate-silvopasture)** — USDA’s 2008 Working Trees brochure gives 50 percent / 70 percent figures for cold effect and wind velocity, clearly dated. Three crops, one fence line, and more management, not less.
