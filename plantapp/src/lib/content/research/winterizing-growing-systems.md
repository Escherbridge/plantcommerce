**Winterizing high tunnels, irrigation, and containers**

# Winterizing high tunnels, irrigation, and containers

A hoop house can burn a crop and freeze a pipe in the same week. Official cold-climate pages already say a tunnel can overheat on a March day and still damage a crop on a cold night, that irrigation lines need to be drained or blown out, and that porous clay pots belong indoors — and they stop there. Frozen pipes and cracked clay are official problems. A hydroponic-tank antifreeze recipe is not, in the pages opened for this guide, and this page does not invent one.

## The structure, labeled USU and Penn State

Bridgette Black and Dan Drost, Utah State University, “Temperature Management in High Tunnels and Greenhouses in the Intermountain West,” April 2010, already used in the [ventilation and heat](/guides/greenhouse-ventilation-heat) guide: unheated structures can get dangerously hot on a clear late-winter day if they are not vented. In a Northern Utah high tunnel, “temperatures have exceeded 120°F on a sunny day in early March when high and low tunnels were not properly ventilated.” Close up for night-time heat, and vent when the sun is high. This page does not invent a Boise set-point calendar beyond what that publication prints.

Penn State Extension, “High Tunnel Production,” updated 13 March 2023: early planting and winter greens are possible with row covers or a tunnel-within-a-tunnel; “sunlight is the limiting factor to how early a crop can be started. Until about the middle of February or beginning of March, there is insufficient sunlight for crop growth regardless of how warm it is in a protected structure.” “Most crops in high tunnels can be damaged if air temperatures drop below 45°F.” Monitor weather and be ready to provide supplemental heat if needed “to keep plants growing actively from the time they are planted.” Portable or installed heat is discussed as a grower choice, not as an Aevani product. Mid-winter light, as a measured daily total, belongs on the [daily light integral](/guides/indoor-lighting-dli) guide. This page does not invent a Boise winter harvest calendar.

## Irrigation lines and containers

Utah State University Extension, “Irrigation System Maintenance,” published July 2021, peer-reviewed, by Candace Schaible, Kelly Kopp, and Jennie Hoover: prepare the system for winter by turning the irrigation controller off; “water should be drained from the system to protect any components that could freeze. Your system may have drain valves that can be opened for drainage, or you may have to blow out the system using pressurized air. If pressurized air is required, you may wish to have your irrigation system blown out by an irrigation professional.” That is garden and landscape irrigation maintenance, and this page says so.

Ohio State University Extension HYG-1647-2000, *Container Vegetable Gardening*, Karen Demboski, Annette Swanberg, and Jane C. Martin, already used in the [container gardening](/guides/container-gardening) guide: “Most non-porous containers may be left outside over the winter, although you may want to store them” to keep them clean. “Porous clay containers should be brought inside to prevent cracking due to freezing temperatures.”

The USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map (2023) tells you the average annual extreme minimum temperature, not when frost will come this spring. That distinction is on the [frost dates and hardiness](/guides/frost-dates-hardiness) guide, not reprinted here. Climate around a closed loop is on the [Intermountain hydroponics](/guides/hydroponics-intermountain) page; the structure itself is on [high tunnels](/guides/high-tunnels-hoop-houses).

## What we did not find

No official page opened for this brief gave a drain-and-store procedure for a nutrient-film or deep-water reservoir, or a safe antifreeze for a hydroponic tank. This page does not invent one, and it does not fill the hole with a blog recipe. A grower still has to keep pipes from bursting; that is the irrigation-maintenance point, not a chemistry recipe.

## Common mistakes

Leaving a tunnel closed on a sunny late-winter day is the first (USU). Heating a mid-January crop that does not have enough light is the second (Penn State). Not draining irrigation is the third (USU). Leaving porous clay outside is the fourth (Ohio). Inventing a nutrient-tank antifreeze, or a “just leave the DWC running under a dropcloth” protocol, is the fifth.

## What to do with the usual questions

**How do I winterize a greenhouse, drip irrigation, or pots?** Official answers: vent and close the structure as the USU and Penn State pages describe; drain or blow out irrigation; bring clay indoors.

**Can I grow lettuce in an unheated tunnel all winter?** Penn State discusses winter greens with inner covers, and also says mid-winter sunlight can be the limit, not only temperature. The lighting guide is the DLI sibling. This page does not invent a Boise winter harvest calendar.

**How do I winterize my hydroponic tank?** Not answered on the official pages opened for this brief. This page does not invent a procedure.
