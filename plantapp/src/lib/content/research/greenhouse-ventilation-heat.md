**Greenhouse and high-tunnel ventilation and heat**

# Greenhouse and high-tunnel ventilation and heat

The sun does not wait for you to get home.

A plastic structure collects heat faster than most people plan for. Official Intermountain and mid-Atlantic guides already treat ventilation as a daily job, and in a dry climate the cooling water can rival the irrigation water. The structure itself is covered in the [high tunnels and hoop houses](/guides/high-tunnels-hoop-houses) guide. This page is the mechanism: air exchange, shade, and the risk of treating a hoop like a sealed box.

## Hero figures

- **Utah State University Extension (April 2010):** in a Northern Utah high tunnel, “temperatures have exceeded 120°F on a sunny day in early March when high and low tunnels were not properly ventilated.” High tunnels in that guidance are usually passively heated and cooled; ventilation on sunny days is required to stay below a crop’s heat-injury range.
- **Penn State Extension (updated 13 March 2023):** “Maintaining optimum growing conditions inside the high tunnel without having extreme temperature fluctuations is critical.” Internal temperatures “should be checked several times a day and the roll-up sides adjusted accordingly.” “Most crops in high tunnels can be damaged if air temperatures drop below 45°F.”
- **Sabeh, Giacomelli, and Kubota (2011), Tucson, Arizona, March–October 2006:** in a single-span polyethylene greenhouse of hydroponic tomatoes, eight-month irrigation water use was 780 L/m² and pad-and-fan cooling used 1,450 L/m² — more water on cooling than on the crop. That is a published semi-arid analog, one house and one season, not a Boise trial and not an Aevani result.

## Daily operation

Penn State’s job description is to measure and move the sides. Many growers add automated vents, fans, heaters, and roll-up sides. Be ready to provide supplemental heat for very early plantings. Air movement also lowers humidity and helps with powdery mildew and tomato leaf mold. Checking the temperature once in the morning is not the published habit; several times a day, or automate.

Night-time cold is the other edge of the same job. The 45°F damage line is Penn State’s, and it still applies when you are trying to hold warmth. Equating “unheated” with “cannot overheat” is how a March sun produces the USU number.

ATTRA’s February 2026 season-extension publication treats shade cloth as a heat tool as well as a light tool. Example ratings in that text include 30% shade cloth as a common starting point, 30–47% in some cases, and up to 63% in extremely hot areas for shade-loving or heat-sensitive plants. Those figures are ATTRA’s published ratings, not a crop-by-crop house chart. The [row covers, low tunnels, and cold frames](/guides/season-extension-covers-frames) guide is the place for the broader cover toolkit, including the risk of stacking a low tunnel inside a hoop and leaving both closed.

There is no single cubic-feet-per-minute ventilation number in the pages opened for this brief, and this page does not invent one. Official starting points are a way to exchange air (roll-up sides, vents, or fans), a way to know the temperature, and a plan for both heat and a 45°F night.

## Cooling water, without repeating the climate page

If you install pad-and-fan cooling in a dry climate, budget water for it. In the Tucson hydroponic tomato house, cooling used more water than irrigation. University of Arizona CEAC teaching notes also state that if evaporative cooling is used, especially in deserts, water needs may double. Quote the study, then stop. The fuller cooling-water discussion, along with Boise normals and hardiness zones, lives on [hydroponics in the Intermountain West](/guides/hydroponics-intermountain). This page will not reuse unsourced “90% less water” language on a topic that is partly about cooling water. If 2023 hardiness-map updates come up, that climate page and USDA’s own caution are the place for them.

When the question is where to site the structure, [plan the land on PlantGeo](https://plantgeo.aevani.com) first.

## Common mistakes

Equating “unheated” with “cannot overheat” is the first. Checking the temperature once in the morning and not again is the second (Penn State: several times a day, or automate). Sealing a tunnel for warmth and then fighting humidity diseases is the third. Installing pad-and-fan cooling in a dry climate without a water budget is the fourth (Sabeh et al.; CEAC). Reusing an unsourced water-savings line on a page about cooling water is the fifth.

## What to do with the usual questions

**What ventilation does a greenhouse need?** Official starting points: a way to exchange air, a way to know the temperature, and a plan for both heat and a 45°F night. There is no single cfm number in the pages opened here.

**Why is my tunnel so hot in spring?** USU already answered this with a March 120°F example when high and low tunnels were not vented.

**Does evaporative cooling save water overall?** Not necessarily. In the Tucson hydroponic tomato house, cooling used more water than irrigation.
