**Permaculture planting guilds**

# Permaculture planting guilds

A guild is a seating chart for plants, not a force field. Official pages already defined it as a longer-term, layered planting around a main crop, usually a tree. They did not publish a pest trial, a yield, or an Intermountain species list. This page is the guild as a design unit. Layers, site prep, and a four-step start already live on the [temperate food forest](/guides/temperate-food-forest) guide. The official record opened here is short — a two-page Kansas State newsletter and an NC State handbook appendix — and the page stays inside that line.

## What a guild is, labeled by source

Kansas State University horticulture newsletter, “Permaculture Planting Guilds,” May 20, 2024, by Cynthia Domenghini, Horticulture Extension Specialist: “A plant guild is a method of growing plants in groups that mimic natural habitats. The diversity of plant material and their roles in the guild support plant health while minimizing the human impact.” “There are similarities between planting guilds and companion planting, but guilds are typically a longer-term design composed of perennials throughout the landscape. Plants work together at different heights (layers) within the landscape and assume roles naturally including: providing mulch, fertilizing, pest control, attracting pollinators, and other functions dependent on plant variety.” “Plant guilds are an opposing technique to monoculture which relies more heavily on human input to overcome problems such as diseases and pests.” “Pest control” is a role the newsletter names, not a proven outcome. This page does not treat it as a guarantee.

Designs “often begin at the center with the main crop,” typically one or more trees in the canopy. Beneath: shrubs (and vines); then an herbaceous layer; then groundcovers; then a root layer. Quick tips in that newsletter: “Select plants with similar water requirements.” “Vary the root systems to reduce competition for nutrients.” “Choose plants based on their function ensuring each plant has two to three uses. Some examples include: food, medicine, aesthetics, craft value.” The newsletter points readers to a Midwest Permaculture e-booklet for “an in-depth look.” That booklet is not an official source, and this page does not import its recipes.

NC State Extension Gardener Handbook, Appendix G, “Permaculture Design,” by Abbey Piner. The handbook page lists a publication date of 1 February 2022 (AG-831). No later revision date is given, and none is invented here. A fruit-tree guild plants several other plants around the base of a tree, “chosen and placed intentionally for its ability to support the tree and surrounding plants in some way. A guild might include a productive fruit tree, a mulching plant, a plant whose roots slow erosion and hold soil, a plant that attracts beneficial insects, and a plant to block the wind.” Figure G–6 is “an apple tree guild with pollinator attracting crown vetch (*Securigera varia*), a dynamic accumulator comfrey (*Symphytum* spp.), and human attracting strawberries planted below.” That figure is NCSU’s illustrated example, not an Aevani plant list. Check local invasive-species lists before planting anything from an example, including crown vetch.

Kansas State’s printed examples — false indigo (*Baptisia australis*) and clover (*Trifolium repens*) as nitrogen-fixing herbaceous plants; strawberries (*Fragaria × ananassa*) and white clover as groundcovers that “are able to block other aggressive plants from invading” — are likewise K-State’s examples, not a local planting list. These pages do not answer “what should I plant in Idaho.” Match water needs and functions, and check local extension.

This page will not invent a yield, a pest reduction, or a seven-layer Intermountain recipe. Outdoor pest methods belong on the [outdoor bed IPM](/guides/outdoor-bed-ipm) guide. Sheet mulch, if you are preparing a new bed, is on the [no-dig](/guides/no-dig-sheet-mulch) page.

## Common mistakes

Treating a two-page newsletter as a production manual is the first. Hearing “pest control” as a guarantee is the second. Copying example species into another climate, or planting NCSU’s crown vetch without a local invasives check, is the third. Importing a non-extension e-booklet because Kansas State linked it is the fourth. Rewriting the food-forest sibling as this page is the fifth.

## What to do with the usual questions

**What is a plant guild, and how does it differ from companion planting?** Official distinction, labeled Kansas State: a similar idea, but guilds are typically a longer-term perennial design in layers.

**Will a guild control pests?** The newsletter names pest control as one possible plant role. It does not publish a trial.

**What should I plant in Idaho?** These pages do not answer that. Kansas State: match water needs and functions; observe what belongs in each layer. Check local extension.
