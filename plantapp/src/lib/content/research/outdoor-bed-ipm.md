**Integrated pest management in outdoor beds and containers**

# Integrated pest management in outdoor beds and containers

A healthy bed is allowed to have a few insects. Strong soil and morning walk-throughs handle most backyard insect spikes long before sprays ever come off the shelf. Outdoor gardens follow a different pest balance than sealed greenhouses, where beneficial wasps and sticky cards play a much bigger role (covered in [greenhouse and high-tunnel IPM](/guides/greenhouse-ipm)).

## The triangle, labeled Penn State

Penn State Extension, “Steps to Controlling Insect Pests in the Garden,” updated 30 April 2024, Diane Diffenderfer (former Master Gardener Coordinator): gardeners “can best manage pest populations by using a variety of control methods.” Regular pesticide applications “will result in populations of insects that are pesticide-resistant.” The IPM Triangle, in this article’s version: start at the base and move up only as needed. Base = cultural methods: “proper plant selection, keeping the garden clear of diseased plants, using crop rotation, and planting a cover crop.” Next = mechanical/physical: “row covers to create a barrier,” plus “weeding/mulching/cultivating the plants and removing diseased plant parts or the entire plant.” The top of this article’s triangle is the chemical layer, “generally… application of synthetic pesticides.” The article notes that some models place biological controls (predatory wasps, purchased ladybugs or lacewings) at the top with chemicals, and “some gardeners prefer to use biological controls in the base layer.” “The overarching concept of IPM is to present gardeners with several options for the control and management of all pests: insects, weeds, and diseases.” Practice: “a morning and evening walk through the garden and scout for insects (and other pests). Hand-picking insect pests during the walk is an easy way to reduce the number of pests.” Activity “may vary by ambient light,” so both walks matter.

Row covers, used here as a physical barrier, also have a crop-cover sibling: [row covers, low tunnels, and cold frames](/guides/season-extension-covers-frames). Penn State’s [pollination](/guides/greenhouse-pollination) guide already says to remove covers so flowers can be pollinated. Site and irrigation affect pest and disease pressure; those notes live on the [raised beds](/guides/raised-beds) and [soil drip irrigation](/guides/soil-drip-irrigation) pages, and container volumes are on [container gardening](/guides/container-gardening).

## Prevention and tolerance, labeled UF

UF/IFAS Gardening Solutions, “Integrated Pest Management (IPM),” UF/IFAS Gardening Solutions notes: “Integrated pest management, or IPM, is a comprehensive approach to managing plant pests. IPM uses many different methods to cause the least harm to people, property and the environment.” Emphasis is “on the management of problems, rather than their eradication. Pesticides are only one weapon… and they are typically used only after a number of other tactics have been tried.” “Prevention is the key to IPM”: right plant in the right place, pest-resistant varieties, healthy plants with appropriate watering and fertilizing. “Plants in locations not suited to them may be stressed and thus more susceptible to pests.” “Conduct regular scouting.” “Don’t treat by default. Plants with aesthetic damage don’t necessarily need to be treated. Consider the amount of damage you’re willing to accept. Remember that there will always be insects in any healthy landscape, and don’t worry about minor damage.” Start with low-impact tactics: “handpicking insects or pruning affected parts.” “Tomato hornworms can easily be removed by hand.”

When using a pesticide, “avoid using broad-spectrum insecticides. They’re not selective, meaning they also kill beneficials.” “Read and follow all label instructions; the label is the law.” Apply during the cooler part of the day; heat plus soaps or oils can injure plants. Always inspect plants in morning and evening walks so small flare-ups get caught early before broad sprays become tempting.

## Common mistakes

Importing the greenhouse pest list into an open bed is the first. Calendar spraying is the second (Penn State: resistance). Treating minor cosmetic damage is the third (UF). Leaving row covers on through bloom is the fourth (pollination sibling). Broad-spectrum insecticides that kill the insects doing useful work is the fifth (UF). Inventing thresholds or a local pest roster these pages do not print is the sixth.

## What to do with the usual questions

**How do I control garden pests, or get rid of hornworms?** Official first answers: scout on a walk, identify, start with cultural and physical methods. UF names hand-picking tomato hornworms as an easy first tactic.

**Should I spray preventively?** UF: don’t treat by default. Penn State: a variety of methods, with chemicals at the top of the triangle, not on a calendar.

**Is outdoor IPM different from greenhouse IPM?** The step-by-step pyramid matches, but pests and monitoring tools differ—closed tunnels rely far more on sticky cards and exclusion screening.

## A situation like this

A Saturday spray “just in case,” beneficials gone, then worse aphids, never a walk the evening before. Official pages already preferred scouting and hand-picking first.
