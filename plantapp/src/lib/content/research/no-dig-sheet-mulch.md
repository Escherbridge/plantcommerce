**No-dig beds and sheet mulching**

# No-dig beds and sheet mulching

No-dig is a layering method, not a lumber-box explainer. The [raised beds](/guides/raised-beds) guide already covers width, depth, and what to put in a frame. Official extension pages describe this sibling as sheet composting: a light-blocking sheet of cardboard or newspaper, then alternating organic layers. They also say it is a cold process. Weed seeds in the upper layers will sprout, and bindweed will not necessarily die. The useful sentence is that a cold pile does not cook weed seeds.

This page is built from two official sources. It does not invent a third calendar or a single house height.

## Hero figures, labeled by source

- **UF/IFAS Gardening Solutions, “No-Dig Garden Beds,” last modified 7 August 2025,** treats no-dig as the same family as “sheet composting,” “sheet mulching,” or “lasagna gardening.” The method uses “a layering system of compost materials to smother grass and suppress weeds” and “makes an in-place garden bed once the materials have decomposed.” Benefits named: non-chemical weed control if the surface is not turned; moisture retention and organic matter, “a big bonus for Florida’s sandy soils”; a no-till practice; and a method “appropriate for everyone (including those who may be physically limited or unable to dig traditional garden beds).” Florida sandy-soil praise is Florida’s. It is not an Intermountain soil guarantee.

  Building steps in that page: plan a few months before planting; untreated lumber if the bed is framed; mow or chop grass and weeds and leave the trimmings; “four to six layers of newspaper or cardboard,” “wet thoroughly”; “compost, worm castings, or manure two to three inches thick”; a carbon layer (leaves, sawdust, or straw); another nitrogen layer; continue “until you’ve reached the desired bed thickness (eighteen inches to three feet)”; top-dress; “water well.” “Sheet composting is considered a ‘cold’ process.” “If you want to plant before the bed is decomposed, you can add a two to three inch layer of finished compost or garden soil to the top of the bed and plant directly.” “Since this method will not produce any or very much heat, it means that any weed seeds in the composting layers (above the cardboard) will not cook and will sprout in your bed. Make sure your materials are weed seed free.” Maintain by adding a nitrogen layer then a carbon layer.

- **Oregon State University Extension EM 9559, “Sheet mulching and lasagna composting with cardboard,” revised July 2025,** by Shannon Rauter and Len Sherp (peer reviewed, Gray level). Sheet mulching “uses a layer of cardboard, newspaper, or heavy paper placed on the soil as a barrier to smother existing weeds and seeds in the soil.” Use “plain-brown corrugated” cardboard, “not shiny, glossy, or printed”; “remove any tape or staples”; “the layer of cardboard should not be too thick, because that will slow down its decomposition.” Soak the cardboard before application. “Cardboard creates an interface that limits the flow of water and air into the soil,” especially if thick; “check with your local extension office to determine if this is a recommended composting practice for your region.” Cover with “4 to 6 overlapping layers… overlapping edges by at least 8 inches.” “Remove any pernicious or persistent weeds such as blackberry, bindweed, morning glory or quackgrass. Lasagna composting may not smother these weeds.” Lasagna form: after cardboard, the first layer should be nitrogen-heavy “because cardboard is high in carbon”; alternate nitrogen and carbon to “a height of 18 inches or more,” with “a few handfuls of soil between each layer”; “the top layer should be brown, i.e. carbon.” “Depending on your climate, the new bed should be ready for planting in approximately six months.”

The two pages do not print one official height or one official wait. UF: 18 inches to 3 feet, and “a few months before you want to plant,” or sooner with 2–3 inches of finished compost or soil on top. OSU: 18 inches or more, and about six months, depending on climate. This page prints both and does not invent a fall-start calendar that is not a direct quote from those sources.

## How to build, without flattening the two recipes

Mow and leave the trimmings (UF). Four to six layers of newspaper or cardboard, overlapped at least 8 inches (OSU). Plain brown cardboard, no tape or staples, soaked, and not too thick (OSU). Then compost or manure 2–3 inches, and alternating nitrogen and carbon (UF; OSU: the first layer after cardboard is nitrogen-heavy). Height and wait, labeled above. If you plant before the layers have finished, UF’s allowance is finished compost or garden soil on top; the layers underneath are still decomposing.

Do not turn the finished surface. UF’s weed-control benefit depends on leaving it unturned. A later food-forest guide, if written, can pick up sheet mulch as site prep. This page stays with the bed.

## Common mistakes

Shiny, taped, or overly thick cardboard is the first (OSU). Gaps at the overlaps, which weeds find, is the second. Using weedy compost in a cold pile is the third (UF). Expecting lasagna layers to kill bindweed, blackberry, morning glory, or quackgrass is the fourth (OSU: remove those first). Digging the finished surface and bringing buried weeds back up is the fifth. Treating UF’s sandy-Florida moisture bonus as a local soil result is the sixth.

## What to do with the usual questions

**What is no-dig, or lasagna gardening?** Official method: a light-blocking sheet plus stacked organics, planted after months, or sooner with finished compost on top.

**Can I plant right away?** UF: yes, if you add 2–3 inches of finished compost or garden soil on top.

**Will it kill grass and weeds?** It smothers many annuals if the sheet is continuous. OSU says persistent weeds such as bindweed, blackberry, morning glory, and quackgrass may not be smothered and should be removed first.
