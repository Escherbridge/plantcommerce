**Aquaponic system types: media beds, NFT, and deep-water culture**

# Aquaponic system types: media beds, NFT, and deep-water culture

FAO’s three small-scale layouts — media beds, nutrient-film technique, and deep-water culture — all move water from fish to plants and back, but only media beds do mechanical and biological filtration in the same box. People often shop for a layout the way they shop for furniture, and then discover they have actually bought a filtration problem. This page is about layout and filtration, not stocking density or cycling. Those already live in [small-scale aquaponics](/guides/small-scale-aquaponics) and [cycling an aquaponic biofilter](/guides/aquaponic-biofilter-cycling).

## Media beds, labeled FAO 2014

“Media-filled bed units are the most popular design for small-scale aquaponics.” “This method is strongly recommended for most developing regions.” “In media bed units, the medium is used to support the roots of the plants and also the same medium functions as a filter, both mechanical and biological. This double function is the main reason why media bed units are the simplest; the following sections demonstrate how NFT and DWC methods both require isolated and more complicated components for filtration.” “However, the media bed technique can become unwieldy and relatively expensive at a larger-scale.” Media can clog if stocking exceeds the beds’ carrying capacity. “Water evaporation is higher in media beds with more surface area exposed to the sun. Some media are very heavy.”

Irrigation may be flood-and-drain (“adds oxygen to the plant roots and aids in the biofiltration of the ammonia”) or a constant flow. Standard bed shape: a rectangle “with a width of about 1 m and a length of 1–3 m.” “The beds should not be so wide that the farmer/operator is unable to reach across, at least half-way.” Depth: “If growing large fruiting vegetables such as tomatoes, okra or cabbage, the media bed should have a depth of 30 cm, without which the larger vegetables would not have sufficient root space, would experience root matting and nutrient deficiencies, and would probably topple over.” “Small leafy green vegetables only require 15–20 cm of media depth.” “Volcanic gravel is the most popular medium to use for media bed units and is recommended where available”; FAO’s preferred size in that section is 8–20 mm in diameter, washed of dust.

## NFT and DWC, labeled FAO 2014

“Dedicated filtration is of critical importance in both NFT and DWC units. Whereas the medium in the media bed technique serves as a biofilter and a mechanical filter, the NFT and DWC techniques do not have this luxury. Therefore, both types of filters need to be deliberately constructed: first, a physical trap to catch the solid wastes, and then a biological filter for nitrification.” The publication’s small-scale NFT/DWC layout is a “a figure in FAO's manual” with one pump and a bidirectional split, “generally” returning “80 percent of the water to the fish tanks and the remaining 20 percent to the grow beds or canals.” That 80/20 split is a described small-scale pattern, not a law.

NFT grow-pipe figures, already cited in the [hydroponic NFT](/guides/nutrient-film-technique) guide: flow “no greater than 1–2 litres/min” per grow pipe; slope “about 1 cm/m”; 11 cm diameter pipes for larger fruiting vegetables or small-scale mixed plantings, 7.5 cm for smaller leafy crops. Label those as aquaponic-pipe figures. Evaporation in NFT is “very low” because water is shielded from the sun; the technique is “far more complicated and expensive than media beds.”

On aquaponic DWC, plants sit in polystyrene sheets with roots hanging in the canal. “This method is the most common for large commercial aquaponics growing one specific crop (typically lettuce, salad leaves or basil).” “The recommended depth is 30 cm to allow for adequate plant root space.” Canal retention time is generally “1–4 hours.” Aeration is vital; the [deep water culture](/guides/deep-water-culture) guide already covers dissolved-oxygen floors, and this page does not invent a second DO target. A “special case” DWC can skip extra filters at “a very low stocking density of fish (i.e. 1–1.5 kg of fish per m3 of fish tank),” relying on roots and canal surfaces for nitrifying bacteria. That is FAO’s low-density variant, not a general stocking rule. The existing stocking guide already holds the water-quality table and the 20 kg / 1,000 litre cap.

## UVI as the commercial raft picture

The University of the Virgin Islands commercial raft system, described by Rakocy and colleagues, is a research-scale outdoor DWC loop, not a backyard kit. Four fish rearing tanks (7.8 m³ each), two cylindro-conical clarifiers (3.8 m³ each), four filter tanks, one degassing tank, six hydroponic tanks (11.3 m³ each, 214 m² of plant growing area), a sump and a base-addition tank; “110 m³ of water” on “0.05 ha.” The system “can produce nearly 5 mt of tilapia along with 1400 cases (24-30 heads per case) of leaf lettuce or 5 mt of basil or a variety of other crops.” The UVI page restates “5 MT of tilapia annually (harvest at 6-week intervals)” and a raft feeding-rate design range of “60–100 g/m²/day.” Print those figures as UVI trial and design numbers, not as Aevani yields or a home feed recipe, and not as a garage build. UVI also states that solids should be removed before they enter the hydroponic component, and that calcium, potassium, and iron are typically supplemented in that system.

Species and start-up still sit on their own pages: [choosing aquaponic fish](/guides/aquaponic-fish-species) and [cycling](/guides/aquaponic-biofilter-cycling). A layout drawing that looks complete is not a cycled filter.

## Common mistakes

Choosing NFT or rafts “because they look commercial,” then omitting the mechanical filter FAO says those layouts require, is the first. Media beds so wide the grower cannot reach the plants, or too shallow for the fruiting crop planted in them, is the second. Treating UVI’s 214 m², 110 m³ loop as a garage build is the third. Using the low-density 1–1.5 kg/m³ special case as if it were the general stocking rule is the fourth. Skipping cycling because the layout drawing looks complete is the fifth.

## What to do with the usual questions

**Media bed versus NFT versus DWC?** FAO’s three methods, with filtration as the real difference.

**Which is best for beginners?** FAO calls media beds the most popular small-scale design and the simplest because the medium is the filter.

**Do I still need a biofilter if I have plants?** In a media bed, the medium is doing that work, within its carrying capacity. In NFT and standard DWC, FAO says you still build a solids trap and a biofilter.
