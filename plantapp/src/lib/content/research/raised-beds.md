**Raised beds: width, depth, and soil**

# Raised beds: width, depth, and soil

A raised bed helps when it is narrow enough to reach, deep enough to root, filled on purpose, and watered as a bed that dries faster than the ground. The boards are just what hold those decisions in place. Lumber is not a growing method, and a box filled with the same clay you were trying to escape is still a clay garden, just higher. The figures below are land-grant dimensions, not a lumber-yard plan.

## Hero figures

- **Width:** 3 to 4 feet, and no wider than 4 feet if you need to reach the middle from both sides (Utah State University Extension, February 2012; UF/IFAS Gardening Solutions, page opened 2026-11-10).
- **Depth:** USU says a framed box “should be at least 6 to 12 inches high to accommodate the rooting depth of most vegetables.” Beds shallower than 12 inches should have no bottom, so roots can reach the soil below; till that soil 6 to 12 inches deep before filling. UF gives a wider band: depth “should be between 6 and 24 inches.” If the surface needs to be higher than 24 inches, UF says to elevate the whole bed and add a sturdy bottom with drainage holes.
- **Walkways:** USU wants aisles wide enough for a wheelbarrow. UF recommends leaving 3 feet between beds for wheelbarrow access, or a walkway of 18 inches or more otherwise. USU’s unframed beds are 8 to 12 inches above the surrounding soil, 3 to 4 feet wide, and typically 4 or more feet long. UF notes that length has “no one-size-fits-all”; eight feet is common.

## Fill the bed on purpose

USU’s fill options include compost-enriched topsoil, a soilless mix, or a 1:1:1 blend by volume of composted organic matter, a mineral component, and native soil or topsoil. If you are using existing soil, amend with 30–50% (by volume) well-composted organic matter. If the added organic matter is not fully composted, USU gives a nitrogen rate to help it break down: in general, for every 1 inch of added woody organic material, incorporate ¼ pound of actual nitrogen per 100 square feet. That rate is for unfinished woody matter, not a general fertilizer recipe for a finished bed.

UF suggests filling with high-quality garden soil or soilless media rather than problem landscape soil when you want a fresh start on weeds. Have the soil tested; USU points readers to the USU Soil Testing Laboratory. [Understanding soil health](/guides/understanding-soil-health) is the related Aevani note on what a test is for. Neither the USU nor the UF page opened here is a slope-engineering manual, so this guide does not invent terracing dimensions for a hillside bed.

## Water it as a bed that dries faster

Raised-bed soils, in USU’s wording, “warm more quickly in the spring, allowing for earlier planting,” but “higher soil temperatures will increase evaporative moisture loss, requiring more frequent irrigation during the hot summer months.” A raised bed is not drought-proof. Drip or soaker hose is described as more efficient than sprinklers, which “water outside the planting box.” Walking in the bed also gives back the compaction advantage USU describes, which is most of the point of not planting in a path.

## Materials, without a scare story

USU recommends non-toxic, rot-resistant, durable materials: cedar or redwood, composite lumber, concrete block, brick, or stone. Untreated fir “will rot and breaks-down within a few years.” Pressure-treated lumber “is impregnated with copper-based compounds and may be used… however, a plastic barrier should be placed between the lumber and the soil mix.” Railroad ties treated with creosote “may cause physical damage to plants touching the black, sticky ooze” and should also be lined. Raised beds can be a way to work around “high soluble salts, heavy metals or other contaminates, or clay dominated soil types.”

UF describes untreated lumber as safe for edibles but beginning to rot within a year. “ACQ Ground Contact” treated lumber is described as approved by the Food and Drug Administration for food production. Avoid secondhand pallet lumber and recycled landscape timbers or railroad ties that may have been treated with creosote, pentachlorophenol, or, before 2004, arsenic compounds. If you are uncertain about treated lumber, UF says to use a heavy plastic liner. Concrete block “may result in an alkaline soil pH but the effect will decline over time.”

Those are the published cautions. This page does not invent a leaching rate, and it does not referee a legal claim beyond what the two fact sheets say. Cornell Cooperative Extension in St. Lawrence County also publishes a how-to-build page, used here as a third land-grant pointer. Extra construction numbers were not pulled from that page for this guide.

## Common mistakes

Building wider than you can reach is the first one; both USU and UF cap practical width around 4 feet. A shallow box with a solid floor and no access to the soil below breaks USU’s rule for beds shallower than 12 inches. Filling with unamended native clay, or with raw woody debris and no nitrogen, is how an expensive box stays a clay garden. Treating the bed as drought-proof ignores USU’s summer watering note. Mystery pallet wood or old railroad ties without a liner is the materials mistake both fact sheets already named.

## What to do with the usual questions

**How deep should a raised bed be?** USU: at least 6–12 inches for most vegetables, and no bottom if the box is shallower than 12 inches. UF: 6–24 inches, with a different construction if you go higher than 24 inches. Those are the published bands.

**Is pressure-treated lumber safe?** Quote the fact sheets rather than a forum rule. USU: copper-based treatments may be used with a liner between the lumber and the mix. UF: ACQ Ground Contact is described as FDA-approved for food production; use a liner if you are uncertain, and avoid pre-2004 ties and creosote.

**Can I build on a slope?** Autocomplete asks this often. Neither fact sheet opened for this brief is a slope-engineering manual. Keep the advice to width, fill, and not walking the soil, and do not invent terracing dimensions.

## An illustrative scenario

This is a worked example, not a customer and not a testimonial. A six-foot-wide box is filled with unamended yard clay and watered with a sprinkler that paints the aisle. The middle dries out, the edges stay soggy, and the reach problem was designed in with the first cut of lumber.
